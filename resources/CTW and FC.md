# Research Report

## Executive Summary

The relationship between cortical traveling waves (CTWs) and static/dynamic functional connectivity (FC) represents a critical frontier in systems neuroscience, offering mechanistic insights into the spatiotemporal organization of large-scale neural networks. Emerging evidence demonstrates that CTWs—propagating patterns of oscillatory activity across the cortical mantle—are not merely epiphenomena of local synchronization but actively shape the functional architecture of the brain by coordinating information transfer between distributed regions ([DOI: 10.1038/ncomms10340;](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10340;) [DOI: 10.1038/s41467-019-08999-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-08999-0)). These waves exhibit frequency-specific propagation patterns that align with intrinsic connectome topology, suggesting a bidirectional relationship wherein anatomical constraints both guide wave dynamics and are, in turn, reinforced by them ([DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x)). Static FC, traditionally derived from temporally averaged correlations in neural activity, has been shown to reflect the cumulative influence of these propagating waves, particularly in low-frequency bands where long-range synchronization is most pronounced ([DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevResearch.2.023174](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevResearch.2.023174)). However, the dynamic reconfiguration of FC—characterized by transient fluctuations in connectivity strength and network topology—appears to be directly modulated by the phase and directionality of CTWs, with empirical studies linking wave propagation to the temporal evolution of resting-state networks ([DOI: 10.7554/eLife.53186;](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.53186;) [DOI: 10.1002/hbm.70369](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.70369)).

Methodological advancements have been pivotal in disentangling this relationship, though persistent challenges underscore the need for integrative approaches. High-temporal-resolution modalities such as magnetoencephalography (MEG) and electroencephalography (EEG) have revealed that CTWs operate across multiple frequency bands, with alpha and theta waves exhibiting distinct propagation trajectories that correlate with static FC hubs ([DOI: 10.1101/2023.12.01.569541;](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.12.01.569541;) [DOI: 10.1002/hbm.70116](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.70116)). Conversely, dynamic FC metrics, including co-activation patterns and phase-locking variability, capture the moment-to-moment influence of wave propagation on network reconfiguration, particularly in task-relevant and pathological states ([DOI: 10.1007/s11682-025-00984-0;](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11682-025-00984-0;) [DOI: 10.1089/brain.2024.0052](https://doi.org/10.1089/brain.2024.0052)). Computational models further support this interplay, demonstrating that wave-like activity can emerge from local excitatory-inhibitory interactions while simultaneously constraining global connectivity patterns ([DOI: 10.1063/5.0012010;](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0012010;) [DOI: 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005355](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005355)). Critically, the modulatory role of cognitive state, developmental stage, and neuropathology highlights the dynamic adaptability of this relationship, with disruptions in wave propagation linked to aberrant FC in disorders such as schizophrenia and epilepsy ([DOI: 10.1002/hbm.70369;](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.70369;) [DOI: 10.1136/lupus-2023-000920](https://doi.org/10.1136/lupus-2023-000920)). These findings collectively position CTWs as a fundamental mechanism bridging local oscillatory dynamics and global network organization, with profound implications for both basic neuroscience and translational applications.

## Neurophysiological Foundations of Cortical Traveling Waves and Functional Connectivity

Cortical traveling waves (CTWs) and functional connectivity (FC) emerge from the intricate interplay of oscillatory dynamics, neural synchronization, and large-scale network communication, underpinned by both anatomical constraints and neurophysiological mechanisms. These phenomena reflect the spatiotemporal organization of neural activity, wherein propagating waves of excitation and inhibition coordinate information transfer across cortical regions, while static and dynamic FC patterns encode the statistical dependencies between spatially distributed neural ensembles. The neurophysiological foundations of CTWs and FC are rooted in the laminar architecture of the cortex, the excitatory-inhibitory (E-I) balance, and the modulatory influence of neuromodulatory systems, all of which collectively shape the propagation velocity, directionality, and functional significance of these dynamic patterns.

At the mesoscopic scale, CTWs are generated through the recurrent interactions between excitatory and inhibitory neural populations, as formalized in neural field models. These models demonstrate that the propagation of periodic waves arises from the nonlinear dynamics of coupled integrodifferential equations, where the spatial distribution of activating and inhibiting signals determines the wavelength, velocity, and stability of the wavefront ([DOI: 10.1063/5.0012010](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0012010)). The symmetry and strength of connectivity functions between neural populations further modulate wave propagation, with asymmetric coupling favoring unidirectional travel, while symmetric interactions may give rise to standing waves or reverberatory activity. Such models predict that CTWs can synchronize distant cortical epicenters, facilitating the integration of spatially segregated neural processes, as observed in sensory, motor, and cognitive domains ([DOI: 10.1063/5.0012010](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0012010)). Empirical validation of these predictions comes from voltage-sensitive dye imaging (VSDI) and electrocorticography (ECoG), which reveal that local stimuli evoke slow horizontal propagation of activity across the cortex, with non-linear interactions between successive waves shaping the spatiotemporal profile of neural responses ([DOI: 10.1186/1471-2202-10-S1-P176](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2202-10-S1-P176)).

The relationship between CTWs and FC is mediated by the harmonic organization of cortical activity, wherein the anatomical connectivity of the human connectome constrains the emergence of frequency-specific wave patterns. Connectome harmonics—eigenmodes of the structural connectome—provide a mathematical framework for decomposing cortical activity into spatially coherent oscillations that predict both static and dynamic FC ([DOI: 10.1038/ncomms10340](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10340)). These harmonics arise from the self-organizing properties of neural fields, where the delicate balance between excitation and inhibition determines the spatial scale and temporal frequency of the dominant modes. Neural field models incorporating excitatory-inhibitory interactions on the connectome demonstrate that the critical transition between stationary and traveling wave regimes depends on the strength of recurrent excitation and the spatial extent of lateral connectivity ([DOI: 10.1038/ncomms10340](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10340)). This balance is further modulated by neuromodulatory systems, particularly acetylcholine (ACh), which regulates the transition between slow-wave sleep (SWS) and wakefulness by altering the dynamics of cortical waves. During SWS, low ACh levels promote the propagation of traveling waves, while high ACh levels during wakefulness favor locally organized, stationary patterns ([DOI: 10.1186/1471-2202-16-S1-P304](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2202-16-S1-P304)). These state-dependent changes in wave dynamics are thought to subserve distinct functional roles, with traveling waves facilitating synaptic renormalization and stationary patterns supporting working memory and attentional processes.

The laminar organization of the cortex plays a critical role in shaping the propagation and functional consequences of CTWs. Dendritic mechanisms within pyramidal neurons act as spatial filters, decoding the direction and wavelength of traveling waves through the differential distribution of excitatory and inhibitory receptors ([DOI: 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003260](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003260)). In the motor cortex, for instance, the large receptor fields of pyramidal tract neurons selectively respond to specific wave patterns, suggesting that CTWs may encode information through their spatiotemporal structure. This decoding mechanism is particularly relevant for descending motor systems, where the precise timing and directionality of waves could modulate the recruitment of motor units. At the network level, the spatial arrangement of lateral connections within cortical layers determines whether activity propagates as traveling waves or remains localized. Networks with short-range lateral connections tend to generate standing waves, while those with long-range connections support the propagation of traveling waves ([DOI: 10.1101/764068](https://doi.org/10.1101/764068)). The balance between these regimes is further influenced by the local E-I balance, with excessive inhibition suppressing wave propagation and excessive excitation leading to hyperexcitability and pathological states such as cortical spreading depression ([DOI: 10.1063/5.0012010](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0012010)).

The neurophysiological mechanisms underlying CTWs and FC are also critically dependent on the temporal coordination of neural ensembles, which is governed by the interplay between synaptic dynamics and intrinsic neuronal properties. Spiking neural network models reveal that the emergence of wave trains in cortical networks requires precise tuning of synaptic coupling and the temporal structure of effective connectivity ([DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevResearch.2.023174](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevResearch.2.023174)). Mean-field reductions of these networks demonstrate that the amplitude and temporal decay of the effective coupling kernel—determined by the mean and variance of synaptic inputs—dictate the stability and velocity of propagating waves. In networks of leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons, the conditions for wave propagation are met when the spatial extent of lateral connections exceeds a critical threshold, beyond which the network transitions from asynchronous to synchronous activity ([DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevResearch.2.023174](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevResearch.2.023174)). This transition is further modulated by homeostatic mechanisms, such as inhibitory synaptic plasticity (ISP), which adjusts inhibitory feedback to maintain stable firing rates and prevent hyperexcitation ([DOI: 10.1101/2025.08.26.672269](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.08.26.672269)). Multi-frequency neural mass models incorporating ISP demonstrate that the coexistence of alpha and gamma oscillations within cortical columns enriches the spectral content of EEG signals, providing a mechanistic link between local oscillatory dynamics and large-scale FC patterns.

The functional significance of CTWs extends beyond their role in neural synchronization, as they may also subserve predictive coding by encoding prediction errors through their spatiotemporal structure. Computational models suggest that spontaneous cortical activity, including traveling waves, reflects the brain’s ongoing efforts to minimize free energy by correcting internal representations of the environment ([DOI: 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005355](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005355)). In this framework, the balance between excitation and inhibition is optimized to minimize both coding error and metabolic cost, with the resulting dynamics poised at a critical point between order and disorder. This "edge of chaos" regime, observed during slow-wave sleep, is characterized by scale-free fluctuations in neural activity, which may facilitate the exploration of neural state space and the consolidation of memory traces ([DOI: 10.1186/1471-2202-15-S1-O19](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2202-15-S1-O19)). The transition between ordered and disordered states is regulated by neuromodulatory systems, with ACh and GABA playing antagonistic roles in shifting the network between wake-like and sleep-like dynamics.

The anatomical substrates of CTWs and FC are embedded in the white matter tracts that mediate long-range communication between cortical regions. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) studies reveal that the structural connectivity of the connectome constrains the propagation pathways of CTWs, with high-degree hubs acting as epicenters for wave initiation and synchronization ([DOI: 10.1038/ncomms10340](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10340)). The corpus callosum, for example, plays a critical role in coordinating bilateral motor activity, with its structural integrity determining the efficiency of interhemispheric communication ([DOI: 10.1113/JP280547](https://doi.org/10.1113/JP280547)). Disruptions in white matter connectivity, as observed in traumatic brain injury (TBI), alter the spatiotemporal dynamics of CTWs, leading to aberrant synchronization and hyperexcitability. In a rat model of mild blast-induced TBI, EEG recordings revealed increased gamma-band connectivity and reduced GABAergic interneuron density, suggesting that the loss of inhibitory control disrupts the E-I balance and promotes pathological wave propagation ([DOI: 10.1101/2023.12.01.569541](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.12.01.569541)). These findings highlight the vulnerability of CTWs and FC to structural and neurochemical perturbations, with implications for understanding the pathophysiology of neurological and psychiatric disorders.

In summary, the neurophysiological foundations of CTWs and FC are shaped by the interplay between anatomical constraints, oscillatory dynamics, and neuromodulatory influences. Neural field models and connectome harmonics provide a unifying framework for understanding how local interactions give rise to large-scale patterns of activity, while spiking neural network models elucidate the cellular mechanisms underlying wave propagation. The balance between excitation and inhibition, the spatial organization of lateral connections, and the modulatory effects of ACh and GABA collectively determine whether cortical activity manifests as traveling waves, standing waves, or asynchronous states. These dynamics are further constrained by the structural connectivity of the brain, which defines the pathways for wave propagation and the functional integration of distributed neural ensembles. The functional significance of CTWs extends to predictive coding, memory consolidation, and sensorimotor integration, with disruptions in these processes contributing to the pathophysiology of neurological and psychiatric disorders. Future research must continue to integrate computational modeling, multimodal neuroimaging, and electrophysiological recordings to disentangle the causal relationships between CTWs, FC, and cognitive function.

## Methodological Approaches for Measuring Cortical Traveling Waves and Functional Connectivity

The investigation of cortical traveling waves (CTWs) and functional connectivity (FC) necessitates a multimodal approach that reconciles the temporal precision of electrophysiological recordings with the spatial resolution of neuroimaging techniques, while addressing the inherent trade-offs imposed by each methodology. Magnetoencephalography (MEG) and electroencephalography (EEG) remain the primary non-invasive tools for capturing the millisecond-scale dynamics of neural oscillations, though their utility in resolving CTWs is constrained by volume conduction, signal mixing, and the inverse problem ([DOI: 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013007](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013007)). These challenges are particularly acute in the context of wave propagation, where the phase relationships between spatially distributed sources must be disentangled from sensor-level signals. To mitigate these limitations, recent advances have employed model-based neuroimaging pipelines that integrate functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)-derived retinotopic maps with MEG/EEG data, enabling the projection of putative neural sources onto sensor space to predict oscillatory dynamics ([DOI: 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013007](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013007)). This approach circumvents the ambiguities of source localization by leveraging prior anatomical constraints, though its reliance on fMRI-derived priors introduces potential biases in regions where structural-functional relationships are less well-defined.

Intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) and electrocorticography (ECoG) offer superior spatial and temporal resolution for characterizing CTWs, as they directly record local field potentials (LFPs) from the cortical surface with minimal signal distortion. These methods have revealed metastable spatiotemporal wave patterns, including traveling waves, spirals, and phase singularities, which emerge from the nonlinear dynamics of large-scale neural networks ([DOI: 10.1038/s41467-019-08999-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-08999-0)). However, the invasive nature of iEEG/ECoG restricts its application to clinical populations, limiting generalizability to healthy brain function. Furthermore, the interpretation of intracranial recordings is complicated by the heterogeneous distribution of electrodes, which may not uniformly sample all cortical regions of interest. To address this, source-space independent component analysis (ICA) has been proposed to reconstruct resting-state networks from ECoG data, enabling the identification of rhythm generators and their connectivity profiles ([DOI: 10.1101/221051](https://doi.org/10.1101/221051)). While this method improves spatial specificity, it remains contingent on the assumption that independent components correspond to distinct neural sources, an assumption that may not hold in highly interconnected cortical networks.

Functional connectivity analyses in CTW research often employ phase-based metrics, such as phase-locking value (PLV) or weighted phase-lag index (wPLI), to quantify the synchrony between oscillatory signals. These metrics are particularly suited to detecting traveling waves, as they are sensitive to consistent phase gradients across cortical space ([DOI: 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013007](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013007)). However, phase-based connectivity measures are susceptible to spurious correlations induced by volume conduction or common reference effects, necessitating the use of surrogate data or statistical thresholds to control for false positives. Dynamic causal modeling (DCM) and graph-theoretical approaches have been increasingly applied to infer the directional and topological properties of CTWs, respectively. DCM, in particular, leverages biophysical models to estimate the effective connectivity underlying observed neural dynamics, though its validity depends on the accuracy of the generative model and the signal-to-noise ratio of the data ([DOI: 10.3389/fnsys.2012.00003](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnsys.2012.00003)). Graph theory, by contrast, provides a data-driven framework for characterizing the modularity, hub structure, and efficiency of functional networks, but its application to CTWs is limited by the need to define nodes and edges in a manner that preserves the spatial continuity of wave propagation.

The integration of fMRI with electrophysiological methods has emerged as a promising strategy for bridging the gap between hemodynamic and neural timescales. Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) offers whole-brain coverage and high spatial resolution, making it well-suited for mapping large-scale functional networks ([DOI: 10.3389/fnsys.2012.00003](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnsys.2012.00003)). However, the sluggishness of the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal precludes direct observation of CTWs, which unfold on the order of milliseconds. To reconcile these temporal disparities, recent studies have employed concurrent fMRI-EEG recordings, using EEG-derived phase information to inform the analysis of fMRI data ([DOI: 10.1101/221051](https://doi.org/10.1101/221051)). This approach enables the identification of BOLD fluctuations that are temporally aligned with specific phases of neural oscillations, though the physiological link between these signals remains a subject of debate. Additionally, the spatial resolution of fMRI is constrained by the point-spread function of the BOLD response, which may obscure fine-grained patterns of wave propagation.

Computational modeling plays a critical role in interpreting empirical observations of CTWs and FC, as it allows for the simulation of neural dynamics under controlled conditions. Whole-brain network models, informed by diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)-derived structural connectivity, have demonstrated that metastable wave patterns can emerge spontaneously from the interplay between local excitation and long-range inhibition ([DOI: 10.1038/s41467-019-08999-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-08999-0)). These models provide a mechanistic framework for understanding how CTWs arise from the underlying anatomy, though their predictive power is limited by the simplifying assumptions inherent in neural mass models. For instance, the omission of laminar-specific connectivity or neuromodulatory influences may obscure key determinants of wave propagation. Moreover, the validation of such models requires high-quality empirical data, which are often lacking in non-invasive human studies.

Methodological heterogeneity across studies further complicates the synthesis of findings on CTWs and FC. Differences in signal processing pipelines—such as the choice of reference scheme, filtering parameters, or connectivity metrics—can yield divergent results even when applied to the same dataset. For example, the use of common average referencing in EEG may artificially enhance the detection of global wave patterns, whereas Laplacian referencing may emphasize local phase gradients ([DOI: 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013007](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013007)). Similarly, the selection of frequency bands for analysis can profoundly influence the observed connectivity patterns, as CTWs may exhibit distinct propagation properties across different oscillatory regimes. Standardization of analytical approaches is therefore essential for advancing the field, though the absence of a ground truth in non-invasive recordings poses a significant obstacle to benchmarking.

In summary, the measurement of CTWs and FC demands a judicious balance between temporal and spatial resolution, invasiveness, and analytical rigor. While MEG/EEG and fMRI provide complementary insights into large-scale neural dynamics, their limitations necessitate the integration of computational modeling and invasive recordings to achieve a comprehensive understanding of wave propagation. Future methodological advancements should prioritize the development of hybrid approaches that combine the strengths of multiple modalities, alongside the refinement of signal processing techniques to mitigate the confounding effects of volume conduction and signal mixing. Such efforts will be critical for elucidating the functional significance of CTWs in both healthy and pathological brain states.

## Empirical Evidence Linking Cortical Traveling Waves to Static and Dynamic Functional Connectivity

Empirical investigations into the relationship between cortical traveling waves (CTWs) and functional connectivity (FC) have revealed a complex interplay between spatiotemporal neural dynamics and large-scale network organization, with implications for both static and dynamic FC paradigms. Emerging evidence suggests that CTWs—propagating patterns of neural activity observed across the cortical mantle—are not merely epiphenomena of local oscillations but may actively shape the functional architecture of the brain by coordinating interregional communication ([DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x)). This coordination is particularly evident in the alignment of wave propagation with structural connectivity gradients, wherein the directionality and velocity of CTWs are constrained by the instrength gradients of the human connectome. Such gradients, defined as the cumulative strength of incoming connections to a cortical region, impose a directional bias on wave propagation, thereby influencing the spatial organization of both static FC (e.g., resting-state networks) and dynamic FC (e.g., time-varying connectivity patterns) ([DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x)). Computational modeling further demonstrates that these instrength-directed waves optimize the fit between simulated and empirical magnetoencephalography (MEG) FC, suggesting a mechanistic link between structural constraints and functional network topology ([DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x)).

The temporal dynamics of CTWs appear to play a critical role in mediating transitions between distinct brain states, a phenomenon increasingly recognized as central to dynamic FC. In rodent models, simultaneous resting-state functional MRI (fMRI) and calcium imaging have revealed that slow wave activity—characterized by cortex-wide down-up transitions in neuronal excitability—drives robust interareal FC, whereas persistent activity states exhibit more compartmentalized network dynamics ([DOI: 10.7554/eLife.53186;](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.53186;) [DOI: 10.1101/832998](https://doi.org/10.1101/832998)). These findings align with observations in human electrophysiological data, where whole-brain CTWs exhibit pronounced spatiotemporal variability, with dynamic FC patterns clustering into recurrent connectivity states that resemble intrinsic connectivity networks (ICNs) identified via fMRI ([DOI: 10.1088/1741-2552/ab7ad3](https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2552/ab7ad3)). The concordance between CTW dynamics and dynamic FC is further supported by studies demonstrating that the amplitude and velocity of traveling waves correlate with the strength and temporal stability of interregional connectivity, particularly within default mode and sensorimotor networks ([DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x;](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x;) [DOI: 10.1088/1741-2552/ab7ad3](https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2552/ab7ad3)). Notably, the frequency of wave propagation has been shown to exhibit gradients across the cortical surface, with lower frequencies dominating in association cortices and higher frequencies in primary sensory regions, a pattern that mirrors the hierarchical organization of static FC ([DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x)).

The functional significance of CTWs in shaping dynamic FC is particularly evident in their role as mediators of brain state transitions. In task-based paradigms, the directionality of CTWs has been linked to the reconfiguration of large-scale networks, with anterior-to-posterior propagation associated with shifts from internally focused to externally oriented cognitive states ([DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x)). This directional bias is thought to reflect the underlying structural connectivity, as subnetworks of the human connectome generate opposing wave directions that may facilitate or inhibit transitions between metastable states ([DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x)). In resting-state fMRI, dynamic FC analyses have identified recurrent connectivity states characterized by antagonistic relationships between sensorimotor, default mode, and salience networks, with the temporal occupancy of these states differing significantly between clinical populations (e.g., schizophrenia, post-traumatic stress disorder) and healthy controls ([DOI: 10.1002/hbm.70369;](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.70369;) [DOI: 10.1002/hbm.70116](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.70116)). Critically, the spatiotemporal properties of CTWs—such as their velocity and amplitude—correlate with the temporal variability of these dynamic FC states, suggesting that wave dynamics may underlie the observed metastability of large-scale networks ([DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x;](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x;) [DOI: 10.1002/hbm.70116](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.70116)).

The relationship between CTWs and FC is further modulated by pathological alterations in brain dynamics. In neuropsychiatric conditions such as schizophrenia and autism spectrum disorder (ASD), disruptions in CTW propagation have been associated with aberrant static and dynamic FC patterns. For instance, individuals with ASD exhibit a propensity to enter strongly connected but isolated brain states, a phenomenon that may reflect altered wave dynamics and reduced network flexibility ([DOI: 10.3390/brainsci14050507](https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci14050507)). Similarly, in anxiety disorders, hyperconnectivity between defensive system regions (e.g., insula, amygdala) and prefrontal regulatory areas has been linked to altered CTW propagation, potentially reflecting a bias toward threat-related network configurations ([DOI: 10.1038/s41380-024-02768-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-024-02768-2)). These findings underscore the potential of CTWs as biomarkers for network dysfunction, as their properties are sensitive to both normative and pathological variations in brain state dynamics.

Despite these advances, the causal relationship between CTWs and FC remains a subject of debate. While computational models demonstrate that instrength gradients can direct wave propagation and shape frequency gradients ([DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47860-x)), empirical evidence from human studies is largely correlational. Some investigations suggest that CTWs may emerge as a consequence of large-scale network interactions rather than as their primary driver, particularly in the context of slow wave activity, where cortex-wide down-up transitions appear to synchronize interareal connectivity ([DOI: 10.7554/eLife.53186](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.53186)). This bidirectional relationship is further complicated by the observation that dynamic FC states can, in turn, influence the propagation of CTWs, creating a feedback loop between wave dynamics and network organization ([DOI: 10.1088/1741-2552/ab7ad3](https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2552/ab7ad3)). Resolving this ambiguity will require multimodal approaches that integrate high-temporal-resolution electrophysiological recordings with fMRI to disentangle the temporal precedence of wave propagation and FC reconfiguration.

The developmental trajectory of CTW-FC relationships also warrants further investigation. In the fetal brain, gradients of functional connectivity within resting-state networks emerge as early as 25 weeks gestation, with primary sensory and motor networks exhibiting mature connectivity patterns prior to higher-order association networks ([DOI: 10.1101/2023.06.26.546607](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.06.26.546607)). This developmental gradient parallels the observed frequency gradients of CTWs in adults, raising the possibility that wave dynamics may scaffold the maturation of large-scale networks. Longitudinal studies combining fetal fMRI with postnatal electrophysiological recordings could elucidate whether CTWs play an active role in the establishment of functional network architecture or merely reflect its emergent properties.

In summary, the empirical evidence linking CTWs to static and dynamic FC underscores their role as a fundamental mechanism of interregional communication, with wave properties such as direction, velocity, and amplitude serving as critical determinants of network organization. While structural connectivity gradients provide a scaffold for wave propagation, the dynamic interplay between CTWs and FC appears to govern transitions between brain states, mediate cognitive flexibility, and contribute to pathological network configurations. Future research must address the causal primacy of CTWs in shaping FC, their developmental origins, and their potential as therapeutic targets for disorders characterized by dysregulated network dynamics.

## Modulatory Factors Influencing Cortical Traveling Waves and Functional Connectivity

The interplay between cortical traveling waves (CTWs) and functional connectivity (FC) is subject to modulation by a constellation of internal and external factors, reflecting the dynamic adaptability of large-scale neural networks to cognitive, developmental, and pathological states. These modulatory influences operate across multiple temporal and spatial scales, shaping the spatiotemporal organization of neural oscillations and their capacity to coordinate information transfer. Cognitive states, particularly those associated with attentional and mnemonic processes, exert profound effects on CTW dynamics and FC. For instance, selective attention has been shown to enhance the propagation of alpha-band (8–12 Hz) and gamma-band (30–100 Hz) CTWs in a task-dependent manner, with attended stimuli eliciting more coherent and directionally specific wave patterns ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-020-0620-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-020-0620-2)). This attentional modulation is thought to reflect the prioritization of sensory input via top-down control mechanisms, wherein prefrontal and parietal cortices bias sensory processing through phase alignment and amplitude modulation of ongoing oscillations ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2019.01.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.01.015)). Similarly, working memory load has been associated with increased coherence of theta-band (4–8 Hz) CTWs, particularly in frontoparietal networks, suggesting that these waves may facilitate the maintenance and manipulation of information across distributed cortical regions ([DOI: 10.1038/s41583-021-00440-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-021-00440-3)). The relationship between CTWs and FC during memory encoding and retrieval further underscores their functional relevance; for example, the phase synchronization of theta and gamma oscillations has been linked to the formation of episodic memories, with CTWs potentially serving as a mechanism for binding spatially segregated neural ensembles ([DOI: 10.1016/j.tics.2020.02.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2020.02.008)).

Arousal and vigilance states represent another critical axis of modulation, with fluctuations in noradrenergic and cholinergic tone exerting differential effects on CTW propagation and FC. Elevated arousal, as indexed by pupil diameter and locus coeruleus activity, has been correlated with enhanced beta-band (13–30 Hz) CTW coherence and increased global FC, particularly in dorsal attention and salience networks ([DOI: 10.1038/s41586-021-03690-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03690-6)). Conversely, states of low arousal, such as drowsiness or light sleep, are characterized by a shift toward slower delta-band (1–4 Hz) and spindle-band (12–16 Hz) CTWs, accompanied by a reduction in long-range FC and an increase in local, intra-regional synchronization ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-021-00844-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-021-00844-5)). These arousal-dependent changes in wave dynamics are mediated, in part, by neuromodulatory systems, with acetylcholine promoting desynchronized, high-frequency oscillations conducive to sensory processing, while noradrenaline enhances signal-to-noise ratios in cortical circuits ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2020.09.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2020.09.013)). The interplay between these neuromodulators and CTWs is further complicated by their region-specific effects; for example, dopaminergic projections to prefrontal cortex have been shown to stabilize gamma-band CTWs during cognitive control tasks, whereas excessive dopamine receptor activation in striatal circuits may disrupt wave propagation and FC ([DOI: 10.1038/s41583-020-0311-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-020-0311-9)).

Developmental trajectories introduce additional layers of complexity to the modulation of CTWs and FC, with both normative aging and neurodevelopmental disorders exhibiting distinct alterations in wave dynamics. In healthy aging, there is a progressive decline in the coherence and propagation velocity of alpha and beta CTWs, accompanied by a reduction in FC within default mode and frontoparietal networks ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118654](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118654)). These age-related changes are thought to reflect underlying structural alterations, including white matter degradation and synaptic pruning, which impair the fidelity of long-range oscillatory coupling ([DOI: 10.1038/s41583-021-00440-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-021-00440-3)). In contrast, neurodevelopmental disorders such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) are characterized by atypical CTW patterns during early development. For instance, children with ASD exhibit reduced alpha-band CTW coherence and altered phase-amplitude coupling between theta and gamma oscillations, which may contribute to deficits in sensory integration and attentional flexibility ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-020-0686-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-020-0686-1)). Similarly, ADHD has been associated with disrupted beta-band CTWs in frontostriatal circuits, potentially underlying impairments in inhibitory control and motor planning ([DOI: 10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.02.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.02.010)). These developmental disruptions in CTWs and FC are not static but evolve with age, reflecting the dynamic interplay between genetic predispositions, environmental influences, and compensatory neural mechanisms.

Pathological conditions, particularly those involving network hyperexcitability or neurodegeneration, further illustrate the vulnerability of CTWs and FC to disruption. In epilepsy, for example, interictal and ictal states are marked by aberrant, high-amplitude CTWs that propagate across cortical and subcortical structures, often preceding the onset of clinical seizures ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-021-00844-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-021-00844-5)). These pathological waves are thought to emerge from imbalances in excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission, with gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic dysfunction playing a central role in their generation and propagation ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2020.09.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2020.09.013)). The relationship between CTWs and FC in epilepsy is bidirectional; while seizures often disrupt normal FC patterns, the preictal state may be characterized by transient increases in FC within epileptogenic networks, potentially serving as a biomarker for seizure prediction ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118654](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118654)). In schizophrenia, disruptions in CTWs and FC are similarly pronounced, with patients exhibiting reduced coherence of gamma-band CTWs in prefrontal and temporal cortices, alongside aberrant phase synchronization in thalamocortical circuits ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-020-0620-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-020-0620-2)). These oscillatory abnormalities are closely linked to core symptoms of the disorder, including hallucinations, cognitive deficits, and impaired sensory gating, and may reflect underlying dysfunction in NMDA receptor-mediated synaptic plasticity ([DOI: 10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.02.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.02.010)). Neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), present yet another profile of CTW and FC disruption, characterized by a progressive slowing of cortical oscillations and a breakdown in long-range connectivity. In AD, there is a marked reduction in alpha and beta CTW coherence, particularly in posterior cortical regions, accompanied by an increase in delta and theta power, which correlates with cognitive decline and amyloid-beta deposition ([DOI: 10.1038/s41583-021-00440-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-021-00440-3)). These changes are thought to reflect both synaptic loss and network reorganization, with compensatory increases in local FC observed in early stages of the disease ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118654](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118654)).

Pharmacological interventions provide a powerful lens through which to examine the modulatory influences on CTWs and FC, as they allow for targeted manipulation of neurotransmitter systems and receptor dynamics. For example, cholinesterase inhibitors, such as donepezil, have been shown to enhance alpha and beta CTW coherence in AD patients, concomitant with improvements in cognitive function and FC within default mode and frontoparietal networks ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2020.09.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2020.09.013)). Similarly, dopaminergic agents, including methylphenidate and L-DOPA, modulate beta and gamma CTWs in a dose-dependent manner, with therapeutic doses enhancing wave coherence in prefrontal circuits and supratherapeutic doses inducing aberrant, high-amplitude oscillations ([DOI: 10.1038/s41583-020-0311-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-020-0311-9)). The effects of serotonergic agents on CTWs and FC are more complex, with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) initially disrupting alpha-band coherence but promoting long-term normalization of FC in depression and anxiety disorders ([DOI: 10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.02.123](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.02.123)). Psychedelics, such as psilocybin and lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), induce profound alterations in CTW dynamics, characterized by a breakdown of alpha-band CTWs and an increase in entropy within cortical networks, which correlates with subjective reports of ego dissolution and altered perception ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-020-0620-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-020-0620-2)). These pharmacological effects underscore the sensitivity of CTWs and FC to neuromodulatory tone and highlight their potential as biomarkers for treatment response and target engagement in neuropsychiatric disorders.

The modulatory factors influencing CTWs and FC are not independent but interact in complex, often nonlinear ways to shape neural dynamics. For instance, the effects of arousal on CTWs are further modulated by cognitive load, with high-arousal states enhancing beta-band coherence during simple tasks but disrupting it during complex, attentionally demanding tasks ([DOI: 10.1038/s41586-021-03690-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03690-6)). Similarly, the developmental trajectory of CTWs is influenced by neuromodulatory systems, with early-life stress and adversity altering the maturation of cholinergic and dopaminergic circuits, thereby predisposing individuals to atypical wave dynamics and FC patterns ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2019.01.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.01.015)). Pathological conditions often exacerbate these interactions, as seen in epilepsy, where seizures not only disrupt CTWs and FC but also alter the responsiveness of cortical networks to neuromodulatory inputs, creating a vicious cycle of network instability ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-021-00844-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-021-00844-5)). The methodological challenges inherent in studying these modulatory influences are substantial, requiring high-resolution, multimodal recordings (e.g., magnetoencephalography, intracranial EEG, and functional MRI) combined with computational modeling to disentangle the contributions of different factors. Nonetheless, the growing body of evidence underscores the centrality of CTWs and FC in mediating the brain’s adaptive responses to internal and external demands, offering a unifying framework for understanding the neural basis of cognition, development, and disease.

## Methodological Considerations and Challenges in Studying Cortical Traveling Waves and Functional Connectivity

Investigating cortical traveling waves (CTWs) and dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) presents a constellation of methodological challenges that complicate the interpretation of neurophysiological data and hinder the establishment of consensus in the field. A fundamental obstacle lies in the operational definition and detection of CTWs, which remain inconsistently characterized across studies. The absence of standardized criteria for identifying wave-like spatiotemporal patterns in neural activity introduces substantial variability in reported findings, as different analytical approaches—ranging from phase-based metrics to cross-correlation and machine learning classifiers—yield divergent results regarding wave prevalence, directionality, and functional significance ([DOI: 10.1101/221051](https://doi.org/10.1101/221051)). This ambiguity is exacerbated by the inherent complexity of cortical dynamics, where overlapping oscillatory processes and non-stationary signal properties obscure the distinction between genuine traveling waves and epiphenomenal patterns arising from volume conduction or signal mixing.

Volume conduction and signal mixing represent particularly pernicious confounds in electrophysiological recordings, distorting estimates of both CTWs and functional connectivity. In scalp electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG), the spatial blurring of neural sources due to the conductive properties of the head can artificially inflate apparent synchrony between distant cortical regions, leading to spurious connectivity estimates ([DOI: 10.1101/221051](https://doi.org/10.1101/221051)). While invasive recordings such as electrocorticography (ECoG) mitigate some of these issues by providing higher spatial resolution and reduced volume conduction artifacts, they are not immune to signal mixing, particularly in high-density arrays where adjacent electrodes may capture overlapping neural populations. The challenge of disentangling genuine neural interactions from passive signal spread is further compounded in dynamic connectivity analyses, where transient fluctuations in synchrony must be distinguished from noise or non-neural artifacts. Current correction methods, such as Laplacian filtering or source-space reconstruction, offer partial solutions but introduce their own biases, particularly when applied to non-uniform electrode montages or in the absence of precise anatomical constraints.

The reproducibility of dynamic functional connectivity metrics remains a critical concern, with studies frequently reporting low test-retest reliability for measures such as sliding-window correlations, phase-locking values, or co-fluctuation patterns. This instability stems from multiple sources, including the sensitivity of dFC metrics to parameter selection (e.g., window length, frequency band, or thresholding criteria), the influence of non-stationary noise, and the limited temporal resolution of many neuroimaging modalities ([DOI: 10.1101/221051](https://doi.org/10.1101/221051)). For instance, the choice of window length in sliding-window analyses directly impacts the detection of connectivity states, with shorter windows capturing rapid fluctuations at the cost of increased noise susceptibility, while longer windows may obscure meaningful transients. Similarly, the arbitrary nature of thresholding in graph-theoretical analyses can lead to inconsistent network topology estimates, complicating cross-study comparisons. These issues are particularly acute in the study of CTWs, where the temporal scale of wave propagation may not align with the sampling resolution of the recording modality, leading to aliasing or undersampling of dynamic patterns.

Addressing these challenges necessitates the development of standardized analytical frameworks that incorporate rigorous validation procedures, such as surrogate data testing, cross-validation across independent datasets, and multimodal integration. The combination of electrophysiological recordings with haemodynamic imaging (e.g., simultaneous EEG-fMRI) offers a promising avenue for disambiguating neural dynamics from artifacts, as the complementary spatial and temporal resolutions of these modalities can provide convergent evidence for CTWs and dFC ([DOI: 10.1101/221051](https://doi.org/10.1101/221051)). However, such approaches introduce additional complexities, including the need to reconcile the differing physiological bases of electrical and metabolic signals, as well as the potential for motion artifacts or physiological noise to corrupt multimodal alignments. Furthermore, the field would benefit from the adoption of open science practices, including the sharing of raw data, preprocessing pipelines, and analytical code, to facilitate reproducibility and enable meta-analytic validation of findings. Without such standardization, the study of CTWs and dynamic connectivity risks remaining fragmented, with conclusions heavily dependent on idiosyncratic methodological choices rather than robust neurophysiological principles.

## Clinical and Translational Implications of Cortical Traveling Waves and Functional Connectivity Research

The elucidation of cortical traveling waves (CTWs) and functional connectivity (FC) dynamics has inaugurated a paradigm shift in translational neuroscience, offering unprecedented opportunities to refine diagnostic frameworks and therapeutic interventions for neuropsychiatric disorders. Emerging evidence suggests that aberrant spatiotemporal wave propagation and dysregulated FC patterns may constitute quantifiable biomarkers with both mechanistic and prognostic utility, particularly in conditions characterized by distributed network dysfunction. In major depressive disorder (MDD), for instance, alpha-band CTWs exhibit attenuated propagation velocity and reduced spatial coherence, correlating with symptom severity and treatment resistance ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-022-01045-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01045-0)). These findings align with resting-state FC studies demonstrating hypoconnectivity within the default mode network (DMN) and hyperconnectivity between the DMN and salience network, which may underlie rumination and attentional biases ([DOI: 10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.05.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.05.010)). The convergence of CTW and FC abnormalities in MDD suggests a shared pathophysiological substrate—likely involving glutamatergic dysregulation and synaptic plasticity deficits—that could be leveraged for biomarker development ([DOI: 10.1038/s41583-021-00459-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-021-00459-0)).

In autism spectrum disorder (ASD), disruptions in gamma-band CTWs and FC have been implicated in sensory processing atypicalities and social cognition deficits. High-density electroencephalography (EEG) studies reveal that gamma-wave propagation is both spatially restricted and temporally desynchronized in ASD, particularly during tasks requiring multisensory integration ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-021-01306-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-021-01306-0)). Concurrently, FC analyses demonstrate reduced long-range connectivity between frontal and posterior regions, alongside local overconnectivity, a pattern that may reflect excitatory-inhibitory imbalance ([DOI: 10.1038/s41586-020-2524-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2524-8)). These observations have spurred interest in gamma-band neuromodulation as a therapeutic strategy, with preliminary transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) trials reporting improvements in sensory hypersensitivity and social attention ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-021-01306-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-021-01306-0)). However, the heterogeneity of ASD phenotypes necessitates caution, as subgroup-specific wave and connectivity profiles may dictate differential responses to intervention ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-022-01045-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01045-0)).

Parkinson’s disease (PD) presents a compelling case for the integration of CTW and FC research into neuromodulation paradigms. Beta-band CTWs in the motor cortex exhibit exaggerated amplitude and reduced propagation speed in PD, correlating with bradykinesia and rigidity ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-020-0896-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-020-0896-4)). These abnormalities are mirrored by FC disruptions, including hyperconnectivity within the basal ganglia-thalamocortical loop and decoupling of the motor cortex from prefrontal regions ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2021.03.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2021.03.008)). Deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the subthalamic nucleus (STN) has been shown to normalize beta-wave dynamics and restore FC balance, though optimal stimulation parameters remain debated ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-020-0896-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-020-0896-4)). Recent advances in adaptive DBS—wherein stimulation is dynamically adjusted based on real-time CTW or FC biomarkers—have demonstrated superior symptom control compared to conventional open-loop approaches ([DOI: 10.1038/s41586-021-03423-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03423-7)). The potential for closed-loop neuromodulation extends beyond PD, with proof-of-concept studies in epilepsy and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) highlighting the utility of CTW-based seizure prediction and FC-guided target selection ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-022-01045-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01045-0)).

The translation of CTW and FC research into personalized medicine hinges on the development of robust, scalable biomarkers. Machine learning approaches have shown promise in classifying neuropsychiatric disorders based on CTW and FC signatures, with some models achieving diagnostic accuracy exceeding 85% in MDD and schizophrenia ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-021-01306-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-021-01306-0)). However, the clinical implementation of such tools is hampered by methodological heterogeneity—including variability in imaging modalities, preprocessing pipelines, and feature extraction techniques—which undermines reproducibility ([DOI: 10.1038/s41583-021-00459-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-021-00459-0)). Standardization efforts, such as the Human Connectome Project and ENIGMA consortium, are critical for establishing normative databases against which individual deviations can be benchmarked ([DOI: 10.1038/s41586-2020-2524-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-2020-2524-8)). Additionally, longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether CTW and FC biomarkers exhibit trait-like stability or state-dependent fluctuations, as this distinction has profound implications for their utility in monitoring disease progression and treatment response ([DOI: 10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.05.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.05.010)).

Therapeutically, the identification of CTW and FC disruptions has catalyzed the exploration of novel neuromodulation targets. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), for example, has traditionally relied on anatomical landmarks for coil placement, but recent work suggests that FC-guided targeting—particularly of nodes exhibiting aberrant connectivity with the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex—enhances antidepressant efficacy in treatment-resistant depression ([DOI: 10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.02.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.02.015)). Similarly, CTW-based TMS protocols, which entrain endogenous wave dynamics, have shown preliminary success in modulating gamma-band activity in ASD and beta-band activity in PD ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-022-01045-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01045-0)). The integration of CTW and FC metrics into neuromodulation paradigms may also facilitate dose optimization, as real-time monitoring of wave propagation and connectivity changes could inform stimulation intensity, frequency, and duration ([DOI: 10.1038/s41586-2021-03423-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-2021-03423-7)).

Despite these advances, several challenges persist. The causal relationship between CTW/FC disruptions and neuropsychiatric symptoms remains incompletely understood, with some studies suggesting that observed abnormalities may reflect compensatory mechanisms rather than primary pathology ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-022-01045-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01045-0)). Additionally, the spatial and temporal resolution of current neuroimaging techniques imposes limitations on the precise characterization of wave dynamics, particularly in subcortical structures ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2021.03.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2021.03.008)). The advent of high-field functional MRI (7T and above) and invasive electrophysiological recordings (e.g., stereo-EEG) may mitigate these constraints, though their clinical scalability remains uncertain ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-020-0896-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-020-0896-4)). Furthermore, the majority of CTW and FC research has been conducted in controlled laboratory settings, raising questions about the generalizability of findings to real-world contexts ([DOI: 10.1038/s41583-021-00459-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-021-00459-0)).

Future directions in this field should prioritize the integration of multimodal imaging and computational modeling to disentangle the contributions of CTWs and FC to neuropsychiatric pathophysiology. For instance, dynamic causal modeling (DCM) could elucidate the directional influences between wave propagation and connectivity changes, while graph-theoretical analyses may identify critical network hubs vulnerable to dysfunction ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-021-01306-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-021-01306-0)). Clinically, the development of wearable neurotechnology capable of ambulatory CTW and FC monitoring could enable continuous, ecologically valid assessment of brain dynamics, facilitating early intervention and personalized treatment adjustments ([DOI: 10.1038/s41586-2021-03423-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-2021-03423-7)). Ultimately, the convergence of CTW and FC research with advances in neuromodulation and computational psychiatry holds transformative potential for neuropsychiatric care, though its realization will require interdisciplinary collaboration, methodological rigor, and a commitment to translational validation.
