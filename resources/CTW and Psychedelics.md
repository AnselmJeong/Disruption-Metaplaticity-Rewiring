# Cortical Travelling Waves and Connectome Harmonics

## Executive Summary

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) and psychedelic compounds represent mechanistically distinct yet convergently disruptive interventions that reconfigure large-scale neural dynamics through frequency-specific oscillatory perturbations and connectome-level reorganization. Theoretical frameworks such as cortical travelling waves and connectome harmonics provide complementary lenses to dissect these effects, with the latter offering a graph-spectral decomposition of functional brain activity into harmonic modes that reflect spatially coherent synchronization patterns ([DOI: 10.1038/ncomms10340](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10340)). ECT induces a transient but therapeutically salient disruption of default mode network (DMN) integrity, characterized by reduced alpha-band phase synchronization and altered resting-state EEG oscillatory patterns, particularly in posterior DMN nodes ([DOI: 10.3389/fnhum.2019.00001](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2019.00001)). These changes are accompanied by functional reorganization of intra- and internetwork connectivity, with post-ECT normalization of hyperconnectivity in the DMN and enhanced segregation of task-positive networks ([DOI: 10.1002/hbm.23928](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.23928)). In contrast, psychedelics such as ketamine elicit a harmonic shift from global to localized functional organization, as evidenced by connectome-harmonic decomposition revealing a suppression of low-frequency modes and amplification of high-frequency, spatially constrained patterns ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.06.20.599885](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.06.20.599885)). This disruption is further reflected in dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) metrics, where ketamine modulates co-activation pattern dynamics in major depressive disorder, particularly within frontoparietal and salience networks ([DOI: 10.1162/IMAG.a.936](https://doi.org/10.1162/IMAG.a.936)). While both interventions share a capacity to destabilize maladaptive neural attractor states, their differential impacts on connectome harmonics—ECT favoring broad-spectrum oscillatory reset versus psychedelics inducing localized, high-frequency perturbations—suggest distinct pathways to therapeutic reconfiguration.

Methodological rigor in studying these disruptions is paramount, as neuroimaging modalities impose inherent trade-offs between spatial and temporal resolution. Resting-state fMRI and concurrent EEG-fMRI-TMS paradigms have proven particularly valuable for capturing the spatiotemporal dynamics of cortical travelling waves and connectome harmonics, though susceptibility to motion artifacts and the indirect nature of BOLD signals necessitate cautious interpretation ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.12.24.24319609](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.12.24.24319609)). Empirical evidence underscores the predictive utility of pre-treatment dFC patterns in forecasting ECT response, with responders exhibiting heightened baseline connectivity in the anterior DMN and reduced connectivity in the posterior DMN ([DOI: 10.3389/fnhum.2021.689488;](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2021.689488;) [DOI: 10.1101/2021.03.31.437958](https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.03.31.437958)). However, contradictions persist regarding the directionality of connectivity changes—ECT has been associated with both increased and decreased DMN connectivity in depression ([DOI: 10.1186/s12888-024-06331-9;](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-024-06331-9;) [DOI: 10.3389/fnhum.2020.616054](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2020.616054))—highlighting the need for standardized analytical pipelines and longitudinal designs to disentangle acute disruptions from sustained neuroplastic effects. Future translational efforts must integrate these frameworks into computational models capable of simulating intervention-specific perturbations, thereby bridging mechanistic insights with personalized treatment optimization.

## Theoretical Foundations: Cortical Travelling Waves and Connectome Harmonics in Brain Dynamics

Theoretical frameworks for understanding large-scale brain dynamics have increasingly incorporated principles from wave mechanics and network theory to explain the spatiotemporal organization of neural activity. Among these, cortical travelling waves and connectome harmonics represent complementary yet mechanistically distinct approaches to modeling the propagation of neural signals across the cerebral cortex. These frameworks are grounded in the biophysical properties of neural tissue and the structural constraints imposed by the connectome, offering a mathematically rigorous account of how local synaptic interactions give rise to globally coherent patterns of activity.

Cortical travelling waves emerge as a fundamental feature of neural dynamics, characterized by the systematic propagation of phase gradients across the cortical surface ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.11.22.624908](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.11.22.624908)). These waves are not merely epiphenomena of local circuit activity but reflect the interplay between synaptic coupling, intrinsic neuronal properties, and the geometric organization of the cortex. The syncytial mesh model provides a biophysical framework for understanding these phenomena, positing that astrocytic syncytia—gap-junction-coupled glial networks—form a substrate capable of supporting damped wave propagation ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.11.22.624908](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.11.22.624908)). This model extends traditional neural field theories by incorporating a small-world astrocytic network, which, when coupled with local excitatory-inhibitory dynamics, generates scale-dependent coherence in the delta-theta range (1–8 Hz). Numerical simulations employing a 9-point isotropic Laplacian and perfectly matched layer (PML) boundary conditions demonstrate that such a system can produce radial phase gradients and interference-driven resonances, suggesting that travelling waves may arise from the interaction between structural connectivity and non-neuronal substrates ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.11.22.624908](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.11.22.624908)). Critically, this framework accounts for non-local reorganization of activity across spatially disconnected regions, a phenomenon that conventional connectome-based models fail to explain.

In parallel, the connectome harmonics framework introduced by Atasoy et al. (2016) offers a spectral decomposition of brain activity that directly links structural connectivity to functional dynamics ([DOI: 10.1038/ncomms10340](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10340)). This approach extends the Fourier basis to the human connectome, treating the cortical surface as a manifold whose eigenmodes—termed connectome harmonics—emerge from the graph Laplacian of the structural network. These harmonics represent standing wave patterns that span the cortex, with their spatial frequency determined by the underlying connectivity. Empirical validation of this model demonstrates that resting-state functional networks correspond to specific subsets of connectome harmonics, particularly those with low spatial frequencies ([DOI: 10.1038/ncomms10340](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10340)). The framework further posits a self-organizing mechanism whereby excitatory-inhibitory interactions on the connectome give rise to these harmonic patterns, with the delicate balance between excitation and inhibition determining the stability and frequency of the observed modes. This aligns with earlier theoretical work on neural field models, which predict that spatially extended patterns of activity can emerge from local synaptic interactions when constrained by anatomical connectivity ([DOI: 10.1038/ncomms10340](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10340)).

While both frameworks emphasize the role of structural connectivity in shaping large-scale dynamics, they differ in their treatment of wave propagation. Cortical travelling waves are inherently dynamic, with phase gradients evolving over time, whereas connectome harmonics represent stationary solutions to the wave equation on the connectome. The syncytial mesh model bridges this gap by proposing that astrocytic networks facilitate the emergence of travelling waves through interference and resonance, thereby introducing a temporal dimension to the harmonic decomposition ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.11.22.624908](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.11.22.624908)). This suggests that connectome harmonics may not be purely static but could instead reflect the time-averaged manifestation of travelling wave interactions. However, the relationship between these two frameworks remains an active area of investigation, as the biophysical mechanisms underlying astrocytic contributions to neural dynamics are still being elucidated.

The mathematical formalism underpinning these models is rooted in the theory of partial differential equations on graphs and manifolds. For connectome harmonics, the graph Laplacian of the structural connectome serves as the operator whose eigenfunctions define the harmonic modes ([DOI: 10.1038/ncomms10340](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10340)). This approach is analogous to the use of the Laplace-Beltrami operator in classical physics to describe wave phenomena on curved surfaces. In the syncytial mesh model, the damped wave equation on a small-world network governs the propagation of activity, with the astrocytic syncytium acting as a medium for wave transmission ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.11.22.624908](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.11.22.624908)). Both frameworks leverage numerical methods—such as Runge-Kutta integration and perfectly matched layers—to simulate the spatiotemporal evolution of neural activity, though their respective emphases on structural versus non-neuronal substrates highlight distinct but potentially complementary mechanisms.

The functional implications of these models are profound, as they provide a principled link between anatomy and dynamics. Connectome harmonics, for instance, offer a spectral basis for decomposing functional networks into their constituent modes, enabling the quantification of network complexity and the identification of frequency-specific contributions to cognition ([DOI: 10.1038/ncomms10340](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10340)). Similarly, the syncytial mesh model suggests that travelling waves may facilitate long-range communication by establishing phase gradients that coordinate activity across distant cortical regions ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.11.22.624908](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.11.22.624908)). These frameworks also raise questions about the role of inhibition in shaping large-scale patterns, as both models predict that the balance between excitation and inhibition is critical for the emergence of stable wave dynamics. Disruptions to this balance—such as those observed in neuropsychiatric disorders—may therefore manifest as alterations in the spatial or temporal organization of cortical activity.

Despite their strengths, these models are not without limitations. The connectome harmonics framework, while mathematically elegant, assumes that functional networks can be fully described by a linear superposition of harmonic modes, an assumption that may not hold in the presence of non-linear dynamics or state-dependent changes in connectivity ([DOI: 10.1038/ncomms10340](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10340)). The syncytial mesh model, on the other hand, introduces additional complexity by incorporating astrocytic networks, whose role in neural computation remains controversial ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.11.22.624908](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.11.22.624908)). Furthermore, both frameworks rely on simplified representations of the connectome, which may not capture the full heterogeneity of cortical and subcortical interactions. Future work will need to address these challenges by integrating higher-resolution connectivity data and refining the biophysical parameters governing wave propagation.

In summary, cortical travelling waves and connectome harmonics represent two complementary lenses through which to study the large-scale organization of brain activity. The former emphasizes the dynamic, time-dependent nature of neural propagation, while the latter provides a spectral decomposition of functional networks grounded in structural anatomy. Together, these frameworks offer a unifying perspective on how local synaptic interactions give rise to globally coherent patterns, with implications for understanding both normal brain function and pathological states. As computational models continue to advance, the integration of these approaches may yield deeper insights into the principles governing neural connectivity and information processing.

## Mechanisms of Neural Connectivity Disruption: Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT) and Psychedelics

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) and psychedelic compounds, such as lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD) and psilocybin, exert profound yet mechanistically distinct effects on neural connectivity, disrupting large-scale brain network dynamics through frequency-specific oscillatory changes, default mode network (DMN) integrity, and the reorganization of the brain’s dynamical repertoire. Both interventions induce transient but therapeutically relevant disruptions in cortical traveling waves, connectome harmonics, and the energy landscape of brain states, though their temporal and spatial scales differ markedly.

ECT elicits a generalized seizure that acutely disrupts neural synchrony across multiple frequency bands, with subsequent neuroplastic adaptations that reconfigure functional connectivity. Resting-state electroencephalography (EEG) studies demonstrate that ECT significantly alters oscillatory power and phase synchronization, particularly within the DMN. Following a course of ECT, patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) exhibit reduced delta and theta power in the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), alongside increased alpha and beta synchronization in frontal and parietal regions ([DOI: 10.3389/fnhum.2019.00001](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2019.00001)). These changes correlate with clinical response, suggesting that ECT’s antidepressant effects may stem from the normalization of aberrant low-frequency oscillations that characterize depressive states. The disruption of DMN hyperconnectivity—a hallmark of MDD—is further supported by functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies, which reveal decreased functional connectivity within the DMN and between the DMN and cognitive control networks (CCN) post-ECT ([DOI: 10.1002/hbm.23928](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.23928)). This functional reorganization extends to dynamic connectivity patterns, where ECT reduces the temporal persistence of maladaptive brain states, particularly those dominated by DMN hyperactivity, while increasing transitions to states characterized by balanced DMN-CCN interactions ([DOI: 10.3389/fnhum.2021.689488](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2021.689488)). The therapeutic efficacy of ECT may thus hinge on its capacity to destabilize pathological attractor states in the brain’s energy landscape, facilitating a shift toward more flexible and adaptive neural dynamics.

Psychedelics, by contrast, induce a rapid and reversible desegregation of large-scale brain networks through agonism at the serotonin 2A (5-HT2A) receptor, leading to a breakdown of modular network architecture and enhanced global connectivity. This "desegregation" is most pronounced in the DMN, where psychedelics disrupt the integrity of its core hubs, such as the PCC and mPFC, while simultaneously increasing connectivity between the DMN and task-positive networks, including the salience network (SN) and frontoparietal network (FPN) ([DOI: 10.31986/issn.2689-0690_rdw.stratford_research_day.179_2024](https://doi.org/10.31986/issn.2689-0690_rdw.stratford_research_day.179_2024)). Unlike ECT, which primarily modulates low-frequency oscillations, psychedelics induce broadband changes in neural synchrony, with magnetoencephalography (MEG) and fMRI studies demonstrating increased high-frequency gamma oscillations and reduced alpha power, particularly in posterior cortical regions ([DOI: 10.1162/netn_a_00366](https://doi.org/10.1162/netn_a_00366)). These changes are associated with the subjective experience of ego dissolution, suggesting that psychedelics transiently destabilize the brain’s hierarchical organization, allowing for a more fluid and less constrained repertoire of brain states. The disruption of DMN integrity is further reflected in the altered dynamics of connectome harmonics, where psychedelics reduce the dominance of low-frequency, large-scale harmonic modes while increasing the expression of higher-frequency, more localized patterns ([DOI: 10.1162/netn_a_00366](https://doi.org/10.1162/netn_a_00366)). This shift may underlie the therapeutic potential of psychedelics in disorders characterized by rigid, maladaptive thought patterns, such as depression and obsessive-compulsive disorder.

The reorganization of brain state dynamics following ECT and psychedelics can be conceptualized within the framework of the triple network model, which emphasizes the interplay between the DMN, SN, and FPN. ECT appears to restore balance within this system by reducing DMN hyperconnectivity and enhancing SN-mediated switching between the DMN and FPN ([DOI: 10.1192/j.eurpsy.2021.359](https://doi.org/10.1192/j.eurpsy.2021.359)). In contrast, psychedelics induce a more global disruption, leading to a temporary collapse of the hierarchical organization that governs the triple network model, thereby facilitating a state of heightened neural plasticity ([DOI: 10.31986/issn.2689-0690_rdw.stratford_research_day.179_2024](https://doi.org/10.31986/issn.2689-0690_rdw.stratford_research_day.179_2024)). This distinction is critical, as ECT’s effects are often sustained over weeks to months, whereas psychedelics’ acute disruptions are typically short-lived, necessitating integration with psychotherapy to consolidate therapeutic gains.

Methodological advances in dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) analysis have further elucidated the mechanisms underlying these interventions. For instance, hidden Markov models (HMMs) reveal that ECT reduces the occupancy of DMN-dominated brain states while increasing the prevalence of states characterized by balanced DMN-FPN interactions ([DOI: 10.1038/s41398-025-03489-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-025-03489-y)). Similarly, co-activation pattern (CAP) analyses demonstrate that ketamine—a rapid-acting antidepressant with psychedelic-like properties—modulates the fraction of time spent in brain states associated with cognitive control and emotional regulation, with these changes correlating with reductions in depressive symptoms ([DOI: 10.1162/IMAG.a.936](https://doi.org/10.1162/IMAG.a.936)). These findings underscore the importance of temporal dynamics in understanding the therapeutic mechanisms of both ECT and psychedelics, as static connectivity measures may fail to capture the transient but clinically significant shifts in brain state organization.

Despite these advances, critical gaps remain in our understanding of how these interventions interact with individual variability in brain network architecture. For example, the baseline connectivity profile of the DMN and its dynamic interactions with the SN and FPN may predict treatment response to both ECT and psychedelics, though the specific patterns of connectivity that confer responsiveness differ between the two modalities ([DOI: 10.3389/fnhum.2021.689488;](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2021.689488;) [DOI: 10.1162/IMAG.a.936](https://doi.org/10.1162/IMAG.a.936)). Additionally, the role of subcortical structures, such as the thalamus and hippocampus, in mediating the effects of ECT and psychedelics on cortical connectivity remains underexplored. Emerging evidence suggests that ECT may induce structural plasticity in the hippocampus, which could secondarily influence large-scale network dynamics ([DOI: 10.3389/fpsyt.2013.00010](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2013.00010)), while psychedelics may modulate thalamocortical connectivity, thereby altering the gating of sensory and cognitive information ([DOI: 10.1162/netn_a_00366](https://doi.org/10.1162/netn_a_00366)).

In summary, ECT and psychedelics disrupt neural connectivity through distinct yet overlapping mechanisms, with ECT primarily modulating low-frequency oscillations and DMN integrity in a manner that promotes long-term stabilization of adaptive brain states, while psychedelics induce a transient, broadband desegregation of large-scale networks that may facilitate cognitive and emotional flexibility. Both interventions highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting the brain’s dynamical repertoire, though their differential effects on connectome harmonics, cortical traveling waves, and the energy landscape of brain states warrant further investigation using multimodal neuroimaging and computational modeling approaches. Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies that integrate structural, functional, and molecular measures to elucidate the cascade of neuroplastic changes that underlie the sustained therapeutic effects of these interventions.

## Empirical Evidence: Connectome-Harmonic Decomposition of Brain Activity Under ECT and Psychedelics

Empirical investigations into the neurophysiological effects of electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) and psychedelics have increasingly employed connectome-harmonic decomposition to elucidate their impact on large-scale brain dynamics. This framework, derived from graph-theoretical representations of structural connectivity, permits the spectral analysis of functional brain activity as a superposition of harmonic modes, each reflecting distinct spatial patterns of cortical synchronization ([DOI: 10.1038/ncomms10340](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10340)). The decomposition of neuroimaging data into connectome harmonics provides a principled approach to quantifying shifts in the energy distribution across spatial scales, offering insights into how these interventions modulate the brain’s functional repertoire and criticality.

Psychedelics, particularly serotonergic agonists such as lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD) and psilocybin, have been shown to induce a profound reorganization of connectome-harmonic dynamics. Atasoy and colleagues (2017) demonstrated that LSD administration significantly alters the power spectrum of connectome harmonics, characterized by a broadband reduction in low-frequency harmonic power and a concomitant increase in high-frequency modes ([DOI: 10.1038/s41598-017-17546-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-17546-0)). This shift is interpreted as a transition toward a more entropic brain state, wherein the functional repertoire expands to include a broader distribution of harmonic modes. The increased energy in high-frequency harmonics suggests a desynchronization of large-scale cortical networks, aligning with the phenomenological reports of ego dissolution and heightened cognitive flexibility under psychedelics ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2018.05.022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2018.05.022)). Complementary magnetoencephalography (MEG) studies have revealed that this harmonic reorganization is accompanied by a disruption of cortical travelling waves—spatiotemporal patterns of phase propagation that typically exhibit stable, low-dimensional dynamics in the resting state ([DOI: 10.1016/j.celrep.2021.109159](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2021.109159)). Under LSD, these waves become more fragmented and exhibit reduced directional coherence, further supporting the hypothesis that psychedelics destabilize canonical network hierarchies ([DOI: 10.1038/s41598-017-17546-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-17546-0)).

In contrast, ECT exerts a distinct, though equally profound, influence on connectome-harmonic dynamics. Functional MRI (fMRI) studies have documented a marked increase in the power of low-frequency connectome harmonics following ECT, particularly in individuals with treatment-resistant depression ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-022-01906-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-01906-4)). This shift is associated with a re-synchronization of large-scale networks, including the default mode network (DMN) and frontoparietal control network, which often exhibit hyperconnectivity in depressive states ([DOI: 10.1016/j.biopsych.2020.09.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2020.09.014)). The enhanced low-frequency harmonic power may reflect a normalization of aberrant neural dynamics, as ECT has been shown to restore the brain’s criticality—its poised state between order and disorder—by reducing excessive functional segregation ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-022-01906-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-01906-4)). Electroencephalography (EEG) investigations further corroborate these findings, demonstrating that ECT-induced seizures transiently disrupt cortical travelling waves, followed by a post-ictal phase characterized by increased slow-wave activity and a more uniform distribution of harmonic energy ([DOI: 10.1016/j.clinph.2021.03.045](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2021.03.045)). This post-ictal reorganization is hypothesized to reset maladaptive network configurations, though the precise mechanisms linking harmonic modulation to therapeutic efficacy remain an active area of inquiry.

Comparative analyses of ECT and psychedelics reveal divergent trajectories in their modulation of connectome-harmonic dynamics. While both interventions disrupt baseline brain states, psychedelics appear to drive the system toward a more disordered, high-entropy regime, whereas ECT tends to restore a more ordered, low-entropy configuration ([DOI: 10.1038/s41598-017-17546-0;](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-17546-0;) [DOI: 10.1038/s41591-022-01906-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-01906-4)). These opposing effects may underlie their distinct clinical profiles: psychedelics’ capacity to facilitate cognitive flexibility and psychological insight versus ECT’s efficacy in rapidly alleviating severe depressive symptoms. However, methodological heterogeneity across studies—including differences in imaging modalities (fMRI vs. MEG/EEG), harmonic decomposition techniques, and clinical populations—complicates direct comparisons. For instance, the temporal resolution of fMRI limits its ability to capture rapid, high-frequency harmonic dynamics that are readily observable with MEG or EEG ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2019.116320](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2019.116320)). Additionally, the structural connectome used for harmonic decomposition is typically derived from group-averaged diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data, which may not fully capture individual variability in white matter architecture ([DOI: 10.1038/ncomms10340](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10340)).

Emerging evidence also suggests that the therapeutic mechanisms of ECT and psychedelics may converge on shared principles of neural plasticity, albeit through distinct harmonic pathways. Both interventions have been associated with increased expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and enhanced synaptic plasticity, which could underlie their capacity to reconfigure functional networks ([DOI: 10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.02.010;](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.02.010;) [DOI: 10.1038/s41591-022-01906-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-01906-4)). In the connectome-harmonic framework, this plasticity may manifest as a transient destabilization of the harmonic energy landscape, followed by a re-equilibration toward a more adaptive configuration. For psychedelics, this process is hypothesized to facilitate the dissolution of rigid, maladaptive network states, whereas ECT may act by "resetting" pathological network dynamics through seizure-induced neural synchronization ([DOI: 10.1038/s41598-017-17546-0;](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-17546-0;) [DOI: 10.1016/j.clinph.2021.03.045](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2021.03.045)). However, the extent to which these harmonic shifts reflect causal mechanisms—rather than epiphenomena of broader neurophysiological changes—remains unresolved. Longitudinal studies combining connectome-harmonic decomposition with measures of synaptic plasticity and clinical outcomes are needed to clarify these relationships.

Critically, the interpretation of connectome-harmonic dynamics in the context of ECT and psychedelics must account for the brain’s inherent criticality. Theoretical models posit that the brain operates near a critical point, where the balance between excitation and inhibition optimizes information processing and dynamic range ([DOI: 10.1038/nrn3961](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn3961)). Deviations from this critical state—whether toward excessive order (e.g., in depression) or disorder (e.g., in psychosis)—are associated with pathological cognition and behavior ([DOI: 10.1016/j.tics.2017.05.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2017.05.003)). Both ECT and psychedelics appear to transiently perturb the brain’s criticality, but in opposing directions: ECT may restore criticality by reducing hyper-synchronized, low-entropy states, while psychedelics may temporarily push the system toward a supercritical, high-entropy regime ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-022-01906-4;](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-01906-4;) [DOI: 10.1038/s41598-017-17546-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-17546-0)). These observations align with the "entropic brain hypothesis," which proposes that the therapeutic effects of psychedelics arise from their capacity to increase neural entropy, thereby facilitating the exploration of novel cognitive states ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2018.05.022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2018.05.022)). Conversely, ECT’s therapeutic action may stem from its ability to counteract the excessive order observed in depression, effectively "rebooting" the brain’s functional dynamics ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-022-01906-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-01906-4)).

Despite these advances, several limitations warrant consideration. The connectome-harmonic framework assumes that functional dynamics are constrained by the brain’s structural connectivity, yet emerging evidence suggests that functional networks can transiently decouple from structural scaffolds, particularly under psychedelics ([DOI: 10.1016/j.celrep.2021.109159](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2021.109159)). This decoupling may introduce biases in harmonic decomposition, as the structural connectome may not fully capture the dynamic reconfigurations induced by these interventions. Furthermore, the clinical relevance of connectome-harmonic shifts remains correlational; while changes in harmonic power are associated with symptom improvement, causal inferences are precluded by the observational nature of most studies. Future research employing computational modeling and interventional designs—such as closed-loop neuromodulation—could elucidate whether targeted modulation of specific harmonic modes directly influences therapeutic outcomes. Additionally, the integration of multimodal imaging (e.g., simultaneous fMRI and EEG) may provide a more comprehensive characterization of how ECT and psychedelics alter the spatiotemporal organization of brain activity across multiple scales.

## Methodological Considerations in Studying Neural Connectivity Disruptions

Investigating neural connectivity disruptions, particularly in the context of cortical travelling waves and connectome harmonics under electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) and psychedelics, necessitates rigorous methodological scrutiny to ensure the validity and interpretability of findings. The selection of neuroimaging modalities represents a foundational consideration, as each technique imposes distinct trade-offs between spatial and temporal resolution, sensitivity to neural dynamics, and susceptibility to artifacts. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), for instance, offers high spatial resolution and whole-brain coverage, enabling the characterization of large-scale network interactions ([DOI: 10.1155/2016/6952169](https://doi.org/10.1155/2016/6952169)). However, its temporal resolution—constrained by the hemodynamic response function—limits the detection of rapid neural oscillations, such as those associated with cortical travelling waves, which may unfold on sub-second timescales. Conversely, electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) provide millisecond-resolution data, facilitating the study of fast-frequency neural dynamics, but their spatial precision is inherently limited by volume conduction and inverse problem ambiguities ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.06.20.599885](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.06.20.599885)). These modality-specific constraints underscore the necessity of multimodal approaches, wherein fMRI’s spatial fidelity can be integrated with EEG/MEG’s temporal acuity to disentangle the spatiotemporal hierarchies of neural connectivity ([DOI: 10.1155/2016/6952169](https://doi.org/10.1155/2016/6952169)).

Data preprocessing pipelines further complicate the interpretation of connectivity metrics, as methodological variability can introduce systematic biases or obscure genuine neural effects. For example, head motion artifacts—particularly problematic in psychiatric populations with heightened restlessness—can inflate functional connectivity estimates if not adequately corrected ([DOI: 10.1038/s41398-025-03249-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-025-03249-y)). While regression-based motion correction techniques (e.g., Friston-24) are widely employed, their efficacy in mitigating motion-related confounds remains debated, with some evidence suggesting residual artifacts persist even after correction ([DOI: 10.1155/2016/6952169](https://doi.org/10.1155/2016/6952169)). Similarly, the choice of parcellation scheme—whether atlas-based or data-driven—can significantly alter connectivity estimates, as coarse parcellations may obscure fine-grained network interactions while overly granular schemes risk overfitting noise. The recent shift toward individualized parcellation, as demonstrated in studies of suicide risk in depression, offers a promising avenue for capturing subject-specific network topologies but introduces additional variability in cross-subject comparisons ([DOI: 10.1038/s41398-025-03249-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-025-03249-y)).

Harmonic decomposition techniques, such as connectome harmonic decomposition (CHD), provide a mathematically elegant framework for quantifying neural dynamics in terms of the brain’s structural connectome, yet their application is not without challenges. CHD assumes that functional activity can be expressed as a linear combination of eigenmodes derived from the structural connectome, an assumption that may not hold under conditions of profound neuroplasticity or pharmacological perturbation ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.06.20.599885](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.06.20.599885)). For instance, ketamine-induced shifts from global to localized harmonic patterns have been interpreted as reflecting dissociative states, but the extent to which these changes reflect genuine neural reorganization versus transient, state-dependent dynamics remains unclear ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.06.20.599885](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.06.20.599885)). Moreover, the reliance on diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) to construct the structural connectome introduces additional limitations, as DTI’s inability to resolve crossing fibers may distort the underlying eigenmodes, particularly in regions of complex white matter architecture. Alternative approaches, such as constrained spherical deconvolution or multi-shell diffusion imaging, may mitigate these issues but are not yet standard in CHD applications ([DOI: 10.1155/2016/6952169](https://doi.org/10.1155/2016/6952169)).

The interpretation of frequency-specific changes in neural connectivity further complicates the study of cortical travelling waves and connectome harmonics. While low-frequency oscillations (e.g., delta, theta) are often associated with large-scale network integration, and high-frequency activity (e.g., gamma) with local processing, the functional significance of these rhythms under ECT or psychedelics remains contentious. For example, ECT-induced seizures are characterized by widespread, synchronized neural discharges, yet the post-ictal suppression of high-frequency activity may reflect compensatory mechanisms rather than direct therapeutic effects ([DOI: 10.1038/s41398-025-03249-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-025-03249-y)). Similarly, psychedelics have been shown to disrupt alpha oscillations while enhancing gamma-band connectivity, but the causal relationship between these spectral changes and subjective experiences of ego dissolution or hallucinations is not well established ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.06.20.599885](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.06.20.599885)). The lack of standardized frequency bands across studies further exacerbates these interpretive challenges, as arbitrary band definitions may obscure meaningful cross-study comparisons.

Methodological heterogeneity also extends to the statistical frameworks used to analyze connectivity data. Traditional mass-univariate approaches, such as seed-based correlation or independent component analysis, may fail to capture the multivariate nature of neural interactions, particularly in the context of harmonic decomposition, where connectivity patterns are inherently interdependent. Multivariate techniques, such as partial least squares or canonical correlation analysis, offer a more holistic approach but are computationally intensive and require large sample sizes to achieve adequate statistical power ([DOI: 10.1155/2016/6952169](https://doi.org/10.1155/2016/6952169)). The reliance on cross-sectional designs in many neuroimaging studies of ECT and psychedelics further limits causal inferences, as longitudinal changes in connectivity—such as those observed in ECT-treated patients with suicidal ideation—are often confounded by regression to the mean or placebo effects ([DOI: 10.1038/s41398-025-03249-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-025-03249-y)). The integration of within-subject designs, as employed in recent ketamine studies, provides a more robust framework for disentangling state-dependent from trait-like connectivity changes but remains underutilized in the literature ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.06.20.599885](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.06.20.599885)).

Finally, the generalizability of findings across clinical populations and experimental conditions remains a persistent challenge. For instance, the hyperconnectivity observed in the frontoparietal network of patients with suicidal ideation may not extend to other psychiatric disorders, such as schizophrenia or bipolar disorder, where connectivity disruptions exhibit distinct spatial and spectral profiles ([DOI: 10.1038/s41398-025-03249-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-025-03249-y)). Similarly, the neural effects of psychedelics are highly dose-dependent, with sub-anaesthetic ketamine producing dissociative states characterized by localized harmonic patterns, whereas anaesthetic doses may induce global network disruptions ([DOI: 10.1101/2024.06.20.599885](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.06.20.599885)). These context-dependent effects necessitate careful consideration of experimental parameters, including drug dosage, administration route, and participant characteristics, to avoid overgeneralization of findings. The development of standardized protocols and open-access data repositories represents a critical step toward addressing these challenges, enabling meta-analytic approaches that can synthesize findings across heterogeneous studies ([DOI: 10.1155/2016/6952169](https://doi.org/10.1155/2016/6952169)).

## Clinical and Translational Implications: From Neural Disruption to Therapeutic Outcomes

The clinical and translational implications of disrupting neural connectivity via electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) and psychedelic-assisted interventions represent a paradigm shift in psychiatric therapeutics, particularly for treatment-resistant disorders. Emerging evidence suggests that the therapeutic efficacy of these modalities may be mechanistically linked to their capacity to transiently destabilize maladaptive neural dynamics, thereby facilitating the reconfiguration of large-scale brain networks ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-021-01318-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-021-01318-z)). Central to this framework is the disruption of cortical travelling waves and connectome harmonics, which are increasingly recognized as fundamental substrates of cognitive and affective processing. In major depressive disorder (MDD), for instance, aberrant alpha and theta travelling waves have been associated with rumination and cognitive inflexibility, while ECT-induced seizure propagation appears to normalize these patterns, potentially restoring functional connectivity within the default mode network (DMN) and frontoparietal control network ([DOI: 10.1038/s41583-022-00642-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-022-00642-9)). Similarly, psychedelics such as psilocybin and LSD have been shown to acutely suppress DMN integrity, particularly within the posterior cingulate cortex and medial prefrontal cortex, while enhancing global functional connectivity in a manner that correlates with subjective experiences of ego dissolution and subsequent therapeutic outcomes ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2021.08.027](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2021.08.027)).

The role of criticality in brain dynamics further elucidates these mechanisms, as both ECT and psychedelics appear to transiently shift neural systems toward a state of heightened plasticity, akin to the critical point between order and disorder where information processing is optimized ([DOI: 10.1038/s41567-022-01518-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-022-01518-0)). This "entropic brain" hypothesis posits that the therapeutic effects of psychedelics may arise from their ability to increase neural entropy, thereby disrupting rigid, pathological patterns of thought and behavior ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-021-01318-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-021-01318-z)). In contrast, ECT-induced seizures may achieve a comparable outcome through a more abrupt, seizure-mediated reset of neural dynamics, though the precise relationship between seizure propagation and therapeutic response remains an area of active investigation ([DOI: 10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.05.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.05.017)). Notably, recent magnetoencephalography (MEG) and electroencephalography (EEG) studies have demonstrated that the degree of post-treatment normalization in connectome harmonics—particularly in the delta and gamma frequency bands—predicts clinical improvement in both MDD and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), suggesting that these metrics may serve as viable biomarkers for treatment response ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-022-01081-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01081-7)).

The translational potential of these findings is underscored by the prospect of personalized interventions tailored to individual neural signatures. For example, pre-treatment EEG profiles characterized by excessive alpha synchrony or reduced gamma connectivity may identify patients more likely to respond to psychedelic therapy, whereas those with hypoactive DMN connectivity might benefit preferentially from ECT ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2021.08.027](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2021.08.027)). Furthermore, the integration of computational modeling with neuroimaging data has begun to elucidate how specific patterns of neural disruption—such as the suppression of low-frequency oscillations or the enhancement of cross-frequency coupling—correlate with symptom remission in addiction and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) ([DOI: 10.1038/s41583-022-00642-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-022-00642-9)). However, the field remains constrained by methodological heterogeneity, particularly in the quantification of travelling waves and connectome harmonics, as well as the lack of longitudinal studies assessing the durability of neural changes post-intervention ([DOI: 10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.05.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.05.017)).

Future directions must prioritize the development of standardized, multimodal biomarkers that capture the dynamic interplay between neural disruption and therapeutic response. Advances in real-time neurofeedback, for instance, could enable closed-loop interventions that titrate psychedelic dosing or ECT parameters based on instantaneous changes in cortical travelling waves or criticality metrics ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-021-01318-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-021-01318-z)). Additionally, the convergence of ECT and psychedelic research with the study of neuroplasticity—particularly the role of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and synaptic remodeling—may yield novel pharmacological adjuncts to enhance or prolong therapeutic effects ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-022-01081-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01081-7)). Ultimately, the clinical translation of these insights will require large-scale, randomized controlled trials that integrate neuroimaging, computational modeling, and clinical outcomes to disentangle the causal mechanisms underlying neural disruption and psychiatric recovery.

## Future Directions: Integrating Cortical Travelling Waves and Connectome Harmonics into Psychiatry and Neuroscience

The integration of cortical travelling waves and connectome harmonics into psychiatric and neuroscientific research represents a paradigm shift in understanding large-scale brain dynamics, yet several critical avenues remain underexplored. A foundational future direction involves the development of high-fidelity computational models capable of simulating the effects of neuromodulatory interventions—such as electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) and psychedelics—on spatiotemporal brain activity. Current frameworks, while increasingly sophisticated, often fail to capture the hierarchical and non-linear interactions between travelling waves and connectome-based harmonic modes ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-022-01070-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01070-2)). Advancing these models will require the incorporation of biophysically realistic parameters, including regional variations in synaptic plasticity, glial-neuronal coupling, and the dynamic reconfiguration of functional networks under perturbation ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118605](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118605)). For instance, ECT-induced seizure propagation may be modeled as a disruption of endogenous wave patterns, with connectome harmonics providing a scaffold for predicting post-ictal network reorganization ([DOI: 10.1038/s41598-021-94771-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-94771-6)). Similarly, psychedelics’ effects on cortical entropy and functional connectivity could be simulated by perturbing harmonic modes to reflect altered excitatory-inhibitory balance, offering testable hypotheses about their therapeutic mechanisms ([DOI: 10.1038/s41583-021-00518-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-021-00518-z)).

A second priority lies in elucidating individual variability in neural responses to these interventions, which remains a significant gap in the literature. While group-level analyses dominate current research, the field must transition toward personalized models that account for inter-individual differences in structural connectivity, neurochemical profiles, and baseline oscillatory dynamics ([DOI: 10.1016/j.tics.2022.03.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2022.03.007)). Machine learning approaches, particularly those leveraging normative connectome datasets, could identify biomarkers predictive of treatment response, such as the pre-intervention dominance of specific harmonic modes or the phase coherence of travelling waves ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-022-01744-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-01744-4)). For example, patients with depression exhibiting aberrant low-frequency connectome harmonics may respond preferentially to interventions that restore harmonic balance, such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) or pharmacological modulation of NMDA receptors ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-022-01070-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01070-2)). Longitudinal studies combining high-density EEG, fMRI, and computational modeling will be essential to validate these biomarkers and refine their clinical utility.

The synthesis of cortical travelling waves and connectome harmonics with other theoretical frameworks—such as predictive coding and the free-energy principle—offers a third promising direction. These models posit that the brain minimizes prediction errors by dynamically adjusting its internal generative models, a process that may be mediated by the hierarchical organization of travelling waves and harmonic modes ([DOI: 10.1016/j.tics.2020.09.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2020.09.007)). For instance, the propagation of prediction errors across cortical layers could be conceptualized as a travelling wave, while the brain’s prior expectations might be encoded in the amplitude of specific connectome harmonics ([DOI: 10.1038/s41583-021-00518-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-021-00518-z)). Integrating these perspectives could yield novel insights into psychiatric disorders characterized by aberrant predictive processing, such as schizophrenia or anxiety, where disruptions in wave dynamics may reflect failures in Bayesian inference ([DOI: 10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.05.018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.05.018)). However, this integration is not without challenges; discrepancies between empirical observations and theoretical predictions must be reconciled through iterative model refinement and cross-modal validation ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-022-01070-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01070-2)).

Interdisciplinary collaboration will be indispensable to address these challenges. The complexity of cortical travelling waves and connectome harmonics demands expertise spanning computational neuroscience, network science, and clinical psychiatry. For example, advances in high-performance computing are needed to simulate large-scale neural dynamics with sufficient temporal and spatial resolution, while clinical researchers must design studies that link these models to symptom dimensions and treatment outcomes ([DOI: 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118605](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118605)). Moreover, the development of open-source toolboxes for analyzing wave dynamics and harmonic modes will facilitate reproducibility and accelerate progress ([DOI: 10.1038/s41598-021-94771-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-94771-6)). Ethical considerations, particularly regarding the use of personalized neural models in clinical decision-making, must also be addressed through collaborative frameworks involving neuroscientists, ethicists, and policymakers.

Finally, the field must confront methodological limitations that currently constrain the interpretation of findings. The reliance on non-invasive neuroimaging techniques, such as EEG and fMRI, introduces inherent trade-offs between spatial and temporal resolution, complicating the precise characterization of wave dynamics ([DOI: 10.1016/j.tics.2022.03.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2022.03.007)). Emerging technologies, including optically pumped magnetometers and ultra-high-field MRI, may mitigate these limitations by providing finer-grained measurements of neural activity ([DOI: 10.1038/s41591-022-01744-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-01744-4)). Additionally, the lack of standardization in analytical pipelines for identifying travelling waves and connectome harmonics poses a barrier to cross-study comparisons. Consensus guidelines, developed through interdisciplinary workshops, could establish best practices for data acquisition, preprocessing, and statistical inference ([DOI: 10.1038/s41593-022-01070-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01070-2)).

In summary, the integration of cortical travelling waves and connectome harmonics into psychiatry and neuroscience holds transformative potential, but realizing this potential will require addressing critical gaps in computational modeling, individual variability, theoretical synthesis, and methodological rigor. By fostering interdisciplinary collaboration and leveraging emerging technologies, the field can move toward a unified framework for understanding brain dynamics in health and disease.
