# Research Report

## Executive Summary

The Triple Network Model (TNN) constitutes a fundamental framework for understanding the large-scale functional architecture of the human brain, positing that intrinsic connectivity among the Default Mode Network (DMN), Central Executive Network (CEN), and Salience Network (SN) underpins cognitive and affective homeostasis ([V 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2011.08.003)). Within this architecture, the SN, anchored in the anterior insula and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, is hypothesized to facilitate the switching between the DMN and CEN to regulate attention to internal versus external stimuli; a failure of this switching mechanism is increasingly recognized as a transdiagnostic pathophysiological mechanism across psychiatric disorders ([V 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2011.08.003)). This dysconnectivity is evident in schizophrenia, where stochastic dynamical causal modeling has revealed dysregulated SN control over the DMN and CEN ([DS et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1089/brain.2023.0054)), and in individuals at clinical high risk for psychosis who exhibit lower SN state switching ([TAW et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2020.00066)). Furthermore, the model extends to trauma-related psychopathology, where trauma exposure and subsequent dissociation have been associated with distinct disruptions to intrinsic network connectivity, suggesting that aberrant salience processing may underlie the dissociative subtype (DOI: 10.1038/s41386-022-01468-1; DOI: 10.1038/s41398-022-01795-3).

While the TNN provides a unifying theoretical lens, the delineation of specific neuropsychiatric phenotypes relies on identifying distinct connectivity signatures, necessitating a methodological shift from static functional connectivity to dynamic and effective connectivity analyses to capture directed causal influences ([D and A 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcn.2015.01.011)). In neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease and amnestic mild cognitive impairment, the heterogeneity of clinical presentation is correlated with divergent brain connectivity patterns, allowing for the definition of data-driven dementia subtypes based on neuropsychiatric symptom profiles (DOI: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.20479; DOI: 10.3389/fneur.2023.1284227). Similarly, research in obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) and substance use disorders highlights the phenotypic variance of triple network interactions; OCD is characterized by a dysregulated connectivity configuration within the TNN ([H et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-025-02921-5)), whereas cocaine dependence severity is specifically associated with disrupted dynamic interactions between large-scale networks ([T et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpsc.2022.08.010)). These findings underscore that static measures often fail to capture the temporal complexity of network dynamics required to understand disorders ranging from stroke-related cognitive impairment to high trait anxiety (DOI: 10.1016/j.nicl.2024.103655; DOI: 10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2020.09.002).

The translation of network neuroscience into clinical practice hinges on the ability to reliably modulate specific neural circuits, with a growing emphasis on understanding the mechanistic underpinnings of stimulation parameters and network-level dose-response relationships ([G et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011572)). Intervention studies suggest that therapeutic effects are mediated by changes in large-scale connectivity; for instance, mindfulness meditation has been shown to increase connectivity within the DMN, SN, and CEN, correlating with improvements in cognitive and affective regulation ([B et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-17325-6)). Moreover, neuromodulation techniques such as transcranial direct current stimulation can induce local effective connectivity changes in patients with OCD ([H et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2025.01.055)), while distinct functional dysconnectivity patterns in the TNN have been identified as potential biomarkers for suicidal ideation in young people with major affective disorders ([WS et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.4088/JCP.25m15906)). Collectively, this evidence supports the utility of the TNN not only as a theoretical model of psychopathology but also as a roadmap for developing targeted, circuit-based therapeutic interventions.

## Theoretical Foundations and Neuroanatomical Substrates of the Triple Network Model

The Triple Network Model (TNN) constitutes a fundamental framework for understanding the large-scale functional architecture of the human brain, positing that intrinsic connectivity among three core macro-scale networks—the Default Mode Network (DMN), Central Executive Network (CEN), and Salience Network (SN)—underpins cognitive and affective homeostasis. The DMN, encompassing the medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and angular gyrus, is primarily active during rest and facilitates self-referential processing, introspection, and autobiographical memory retrieval. In contrast, the CEN, which includes the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and posterior parietal cortex, is engaged during goal-directed, externally oriented cognitive tasks requiring working memory and attentional control. The functional antagonism between the DMN and CEN is well-established, yet the interaction between these systems is neither static nor purely binary; rather, it relies heavily on the regulatory properties of the SN.

The SN, anchored in the anterior insula and the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, serves as the neuroanatomical substrate for detecting behaviorally relevant external stimuli and internal interoceptive states. The prevailing theoretical foundation of the TNN posits that the SN functions as a dynamic "switch," initiating the toggling between the DMN and CEN to facilitate appropriate cognitive and behavioral responses to salient events. This switching mechanism is supported by stochastic dynamical causal modeling, which demonstrates that the SN exerts causal regulatory control over both the DMN and CEN, effectively modulating the engagement of introspective versus executive systems ([DS et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1089/brain.2023.0054)). The integrity of this switching mechanism is critical for cognitive flexibility; failures in SN-mediated state switching have been linked to aberrant network dynamics in pre-psychosis, where reduced flexibility in network reconfiguration is observed ([TAW et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2020.00066)). Furthermore, the efficacy of this tripartite interaction has significant behavioral correlates, as distinct connectivity patterns within the triple-network saliency model are predictive of real-life self-control capabilities ([KM et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2020.107667)).

The intrinsic functional architecture of these networks is not fixed but demonstrates plasticity in response to behavioral interventions. For instance, mindfulness meditation has been shown to increase functional connectivity across the DMN, SN, and CEN, suggesting that the baseline intrinsic architecture can be modified through sustained training to enhance network integration ([B et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-17325-6)). Conversely, disruptions to the delicate balance between these networks are implicated in a wide spectrum of neuropsychiatric pathology. In conditions characterized by cognitive and affective deficits, the role of the SN is particularly pronounced, as it integrates emotional salience with cognitive control ([J et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2023.1133367)). Dysregulation of the SN's switching capacity is evident in addiction disorders, where altered triple-network resting-state connectivity predicts distress tolerance and is associated with cocaine use ([ED et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm8122135)) and heavy alcohol drinking ([M et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/alcalc/agae056)).

Neurodegenerative and developmental disorders further elucidate the structural and functional constraints of the TNN. In Alzheimer’s disease, abnormal connectivity within the triple-network model is evident, and utilizing a combination of static and dynamic functional connectivity metrics provides superior insight into the progression of amnestic mild cognitive impairment compared to static metrics alone ([Q et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2023.1284227)). This dysconnectivity is also predictive of cognitive decline and depressive symptoms in older adults ([A et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/alz.14493)). Similarly, the developmental trajectory of these networks influences clinical outcomes; pubertal maturation impacts DMN connectivity in ways that are implicated in mood dysregulation ([M et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-019-0433-6)), while autism spectrum disorder co-occurring with ADHD presents with altered functional connectivity specifically within the triple-network architecture ([K et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2021.736755)).

The robustness of the TNN framework is further validated by consistent findings of network decoupling in severe psychopathology. In obsessive-compulsive disorder, dysregulated connectivity configuration within and between the DMN, CEN, and SN has been extensively documented ([J et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2017.07.041)), with recent research identifying distinct dysregulated connectivity patterns in the TNN ([H et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-025-02921-5)). Similar disruptions in network coupling are observed in behavioral addictions such as Internet gaming disorder ([JT et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eurpsy.2017.06.012)) and in stroke patients exhibiting multidomain cognitive impairments ([Y et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nicl.2024.103655)). Even in disorders of consciousness, such as the vegetative state, altered regional activity and connectivity within the TNN contrast with the patterns observed in minimally conscious states, highlighting the necessity of intact SN-mediated switching for conscious awareness ([Y et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2022.1001519)). Finally, environmental stressors, such as torture exposure, induce significant disruptions to intrinsic network connectivity within this model, suggesting the TNN is a sensitive marker for the neural impact of severe trauma ([BJ et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-022-01795-3)).

## Network Dysconnectivity as a Transdiagnostic Pathophysiological Mechanism

The conceptualization of large-scale brain network interactions provides a robust framework for understanding the pathophysiology of psychiatric disorders, positing that the dynamic interplay between the default mode network (DMN), central executive network (CEN), and salience network (SN) constitutes a core functional architecture ([V 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2011.08.003)). The triple network model hypothesizes that the SN, anchored in the anterior insula and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, functions as a causal switch that identifies behaviorally relevant stimuli and modulates the anticorrelated activity between the internally oriented DMN and the externally oriented CEN ([V 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2011.08.003)). Emerging evidence suggests that the failure of the SN to appropriately engage or disengage the DMN and CEN represents a transdiagnostic neural substrate underlying a spectrum of psychopathologies, ranging from psychotic disorders to mood and anxiety conditions ([V et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2022.09.029)).

Central to this pathophysiological mechanism is the dysregulation of the SN and its capacity to facilitate network switching. In schizophrenia, empirical studies have demonstrated that reduced state switching capabilities of the SN precede the onset of psychosis, suggesting that aberrant salience detection is a primary deficit rather than a consequence of chronic illness ([TAW et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2020.00066)). This dysregulated dynamics perspective is further supported by findings of abnormal connectivity configurations within the SN in individuals at clinical high risk for psychosis and those with first-episode psychosis, implicating SN failure as a biomarker for disease progression ([A et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.30773/pi.2022.0091)). Consequently, the inability to suppress the DMN during cognitive tasks leads to intrusive self-referential thoughts and deficits in executive control, a pattern consistently observed in schizophrenia spectrum disorders ([K et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2018.07.020)). While the triple network model offers a compelling explanatory framework, recent reviews of dysconnectivity in schizophrenia note that it coexists alongside other network disturbances, necessitating a multi-faceted understanding of the disorder's functional topology ([A et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11910-023-01325-8)).

The disruption of DMN-CEN anticorrelation extends beyond psychosis, manifesting in affective and anxiety disorders. In major affective disorders, particularly among patients with suicidal ideation, distinct patterns of triple network dysconnectivity have been identified, where the failure to segregate the DMN from task-positive networks correlates with symptom severity ([WS et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.4088/JCP.25m15906)). Similarly, research on high trait anxiety indicates that dysregulated brain salience processing interferes with the competitive dynamics between the DMN and CEN, potentially maintaining states of hypervigilance and worry ([C et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2020.09.002)). Trauma-related dissociation has also been re-conceptualized through this lens, where altered SN interactions with the DMN and CEN underpin the fragmentation of self-referential processing and cognitive control ([LAM et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41386-022-01468-1)). Furthermore, in obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), investigations have revealed a dysregulated connectivity configuration within the triple network model, highlighting that aberrant salience attribution to intrusive thoughts drives the failure of the CEN to exert top-down control over DMN activity ([H et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-025-02921-5)).

The transdiagnostic utility of the triple network model is further underscored by its application to neurodevelopmental and geriatric populations. Testing the model in neurodevelopmental cohorts has confirmed that triple network dysfunction contributes to cognitive and psychopathological outcomes across various diagnostic categories, supporting the hypothesis of a common neural substrate ([JS et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nicl.2023.103539)). In older adults, altered connectivity between these networks is associated with both cognitive decline and depressive symptoms, suggesting that network dysconnectivity mediates the intersection of mood and neurodegenerative processes ([A et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/alz.14493)). Even in conditions involving profound cognitive impairment, such as stroke or disorders of consciousness, the preservation or degradation of SN integrity and its interaction with the DMN and CEN serves as a critical indicator of functional status and cognitive reserve (DOI: 10.1016/j.nicl.2024.103655; DOI: 10.3389/fnbeh.2022.1001519).

The etiology of these network disturbances appears to be rooted in both genetic architecture and experiential factors. Genome-wide association studies have established that common genetic variants contribute significantly to the intrinsic functional connectivity of the DMN, CEN, and SN, providing a biological basis for the observed network phenotypes ([B et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-022-01039-6)). However, the plasticity of these networks offers potential avenues for therapeutic intervention. For instance, mindfulness meditation has been shown to increase connectivity within and between the DMN, SN, and CEN, suggesting that behavioral interventions can normalize the pathological anticorrelation observed in psychiatric conditions ([B et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-17325-6)). The role of the SN in identifying salient internal and external stimuli also extends to somatic conditions, such as chronic pain and tinnitus, where aberrant SN engagement triggers maladaptive interactions with the DMN and CEN, blurring the lines between neurological and psychiatric symptomatology (DOI: 10.3389/fneur.2022.757241; DOI: 10.21053/ceo.2022.00815). Ultimately, the convergence of evidence across diverse disorders supports the theoretical refinement of the triple network model as a fundamental structure of the mind, where the SN’s pivotal role in network switching acts as a gateway for cognitive and emotional regulation ([B 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mehy.2019.109385)).

## Disorder-Specific Network Interactions and Phenotypic Variance

The delineation of neuropsychiatric disorders increasingly relies on identifying distinct connectivity signatures that map onto specific phenotypic expressions, moving beyond diagnostic categories toward biologically defined subtypes. In the context of neurodegeneration, the heterogeneity of dementia is underscored by findings that neuropsychiatric symptoms—such as agitation, depression, or psychosis—correspond to divergent brain connectivity patterns, allowing for the definition of data-driven subtypes through symptom-associated functional architecture ([K et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.07.02.547427)). This connectivity-based subtyping has been rigorously validated in subsequent analyses, confirming that specific neuropsychiatric clusters are linked to unique patterns of neural coherence, thereby refining the phenotypic classification of dementia beyond traditional cognitive criteria ([K et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.20479)). Extending these findings across the Alzheimer's disease continuum, multiomic approaches have revealed that macroscale structural covariance network dysfunction is mechanistically linked to the severity of neuropsychiatric symptoms, suggesting that the disintegration of large-scale network topology mirrors underlying molecular pathology ([J et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2024.08.027)). Phenotypic variance is particularly pronounced in frontotemporal dementia (FTD), where age of onset critically modulates the clinical and metabolic profiles; earlier onset is associated with distinct metabolic alterations that likely drive the observed behavioral phenotype, highlighting the interplay between developmental timing and network vulnerability ([M et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2025.01.011)).

Beyond neurodegeneration, network interactions serve as a critical substrate for symptom severity and behavioral phenotypes in primary psychiatric and functional conditions. In major depressive disorder (MDD), the propensity for suicidal ideation has been correlated with alterations in brain network controllability, which in turn is associated with distinct regional gene expression profiles, highlighting a molecular-translational basis for the most severe behavioral manifestations ([X et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1155/da/6529148)). This intersection of network physiology and genetic predisposition is also evident in Parkinson’s disease, where imaging genetics analyses demonstrate that specific genetic variability can explain the degree of depressive symptoms, suggesting that unique neurobiological pathways underpin affective variance in neurodegenerative populations ([JH et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0211699)). In functional neurological disorder (FND), the etiological role of early-life trauma is mediated through specific brain circuit-gene expression relationships, establishing an endophenotype that links environmental stressors to measurable network aberrations and functional motor symptoms ([I et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-020-0665-0)). Furthermore, schizophrenia research has identified membrane lipid alterations as potential biomarkers, implicating disruptions in cellular membrane integrity as a potential upstream mechanism for the widespread network dysconnectivity observed in early phases of psychosis ([A et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.encep.2019.11.009)).

The temporal dynamics of network connectivity are especially pertinent in substance use disorders, where disrupted dynamic interactions between large-scale networks in cocaine users show a direct correlation with dependence severity, indicating that the flexibility of neural transitions between brain states is a critical component of the addiction phenotype ([T et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpsc.2022.08.010)). Network dysfunction is also a consequence of systemic inflammation and autoimmunity, as seen in ulcerative colitis, where resting-state functional connectivity alterations suggest a gut-brain axis contribution to central symptomatology and fatigue ([AS 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2025.1608371)). Experimental evidence for antibody-mediated network disruption comes from rodent models of CASPR2 autoantibody encephalitis, where cerebrospinal fluid monoclonal antibodies induce specific electrophysiological and fMRI changes that parallel behavioral deficits, providing a causal link between peripheral autoimmunity and central network pathology ([S et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bbi.2024.08.027)). Similarly, in rare genetic disorders such as Niemann-Pick disease type C and CLN6-related neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis, the central manifestations and phenotypic continua are characterized by specific patterns of neurological decline, often precipitated by aberrant splicing or lipid accumulation that fundamentally alters neural system integrity ([R et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gim.2023.101053)) ([F et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/epi4.13119)). Finally, cognitive impairment in multiple sclerosis is posited to arise from a complex interplay of neurodegenerative and inflammatory mechanisms, where the dissociation between structural damage and clinical phenotype suggests a role for network-level compensation that fails to mask cognitive decline in progressive stages ([KA 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00702-024-02786-y)).

## Methodological Approaches in Mapping Functional Connectivity

The assessment of large-scale brain network interactions in clinical neuroscience relies fundamentally on the distinction between functional connectivity, which denotes statistical dependencies between neural time series, and effective connectivity, which implies directed causal influences ([D and A 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcn.2015.01.011)). While standard resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) has historically utilized static functional connectivity measures, these approaches often fail to capture the non-stationary nature of neural activity, prompting a methodological shift toward dynamic analytical frameworks that better reflect the temporal variability of brain states ([A et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clineuro.2025.109055)). For instance, in pathologies such as arteriovenous malformations-related epilepsy, contrasting static and dynamic connectivity metrics reveals that dynamic measures may offer superior sensitivity to local network disruptions ([A et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clineuro.2025.109055)). Similarly, diminished temporal dynamics have been identified as a pathological hallmark in amnestic mild cognitive impairment, suggesting that reduced neural flexibility contributes to cognitive decline ([EK et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2018.05.001)). However, the interpretation of dynamic connectivity is complex; while aberrant dynamic network connectivity is frequently reported in comorbid conditions like depression and obesity, the specificity of these alterations within the triple network model requires rigorous validation against potential metabolic confounds ([ZQ et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/jnr.70001)).

To address the limitations of correlational approaches, Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM) has emerged as a potent technique for inferring effective connectivity at the neuronal level. Historically constrained to small-scale networks, the methodology has recently been expanded to large-scale whole-brain architectures, enabling the assessment of hierarchical neuronal interactions during resting states ([A et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1162/NETN_a_00015)). This advancement facilitates the investigation of directed network changes following interventions; for example, local effective connectivity changes have been observed after transcranial direct current stimulation in patients with obsessive-compulsive disorder, providing mechanistic insights into therapeutic response ([H et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2025.01.055)). Furthermore, effective connectivity analyses have uncovered gender-related differences in frontal-parietal modular segregation in internet gaming disorder, highlighting the utility of causal modeling in understanding behavioral addictions ([N et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2021.00015)). Conversely, studies in non-human primates utilizing graph theory have identified specific hubs bridging sensorimotor and cognitive networks, demonstrating that topological analyses can elucidate the anatomical substrates of integration ([R et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2022.119244)). The resilience of these hubs to targeted attacks suggests a robust structural scaffold for network reorganization, a finding corroborated by simulations showing network reorganization following hub disruption ([W et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118219)).

Methodological rigor in graph theoretical analysis remains paramount, particularly regarding the selection of network thresholds which can significantly alter topological inferences. Novel data-driven thresholding schemes, such as those based on Orthogonal Minimal Spanning Trees (OMSTs), have been proposed to mitigate spurious connections and preserve the topological fidelity of dynamic functional brain networks ([SI et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fninf.2017.00028)). These methodological refinements are critical for clinical classification, as evidenced by efforts to classify the psychosis continuum using connectivity-driven features, where distinct connectivity profiles have been suggested to demarcate stages of the disorder ([A et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2014.01047)). Moreover, the assessment of network segregation and integration has proven sensitive to metabolic dysregulation, with topological dissimilarities in hierarchical resting networks identified in type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity, indicating that systemic pathology disrupts the brain's functional architecture ([SC et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10827-022-00833-9)). In the context of cognitive performance, the flexibility of fronto-parietal subnetworks appears to serve a compensatory function against mental fatigue, indicating that dynamic network reconfiguration is a mechanism of cognitive resilience ([F et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.24192)). Large-scale harmonized analyses, such as those conducted on the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange, further underscore the necessity of controlling for site-specific variances to reliably detect alterations in connectome dynamics across clinical populations ([Y et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2021.12.004)). Finally, the concept of dynamic task-linked switching provides a tri-network perspective that integrates these disparate findings, suggesting that the fluid exchange of information between large-scale networks is fundamental to adaptive behavior ([SB et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bandc.2021.105725)).

## Translational Applications: Diagnostic Biomarkers and Neuromodulation Targets

The translation of network neuroscience into clinical practice hinges on the ability to reliably modulate specific neural circuits, with a growing emphasis on understanding the mechanistic underpinnings of stimulation parameters. In the context of neuromodulation, it has become evident that the therapeutic impact of interventions is mediated by complex dose-response relationships that extend beyond the focal target site to encompass broader network dynamics ([G et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011572)). Computational approaches have been instrumental in characterizing these dynamics, revealing that the modulation of brain function exhibits a non-linear dose-response profile that transitions from local alterations to system-wide network changes ([G et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011572)). This phenomenon underscores the necessity of optimizing stimulation parameters not merely for local cortical excitability but for their capacity to normalize pathological network connectivity patterns ([G et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011572)). The precise calibration of these parameters is paramount, as the efficacy of network-level modulation is highly sensitive to variations in dosage ([G et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011572)). Consequently, the development of individualized neuromodulation protocols requires a rigorous understanding of the specific dose-response curves that govern the transition from local physiological changes to the functional reorganization of large-scale brain networks ([G et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011572)).
