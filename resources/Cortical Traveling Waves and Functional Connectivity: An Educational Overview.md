# **Cortical Traveling Waves and Functional Connectivity: An Educational Overview**

## **Introduction**

Functional connectivity (FC) refers to statistical relationships (often measured as correlations) between the activity of different brain regions. In resting-state fMRI, for example, FC is inferred from the synchronization of slow fluctuations in the BOLD signal across regions, typically at **zero lag** (i.e. simultaneously) . Early theories of brain coordination emphasized **zero-lag synchrony** – the idea that distant neurons or areas oscillating in perfect unison could facilitate communication and binding of information . However, new evidence has expanded this view: the brain’s activity is not strictly in lockstep, but often exhibits **traveling waves**, where patterns of neural oscillation sweep across the cortex with slight time delays . This pedagogical report will explain how such **cortical traveling waves** relate to functional connectivity, and introduce key concepts – from zero-lag connectivity to **principal gradients** and **cortical eigenmodes**, from **metastability** to the notion of an underlying **potential landscape** shaped by these eigenmodes. Our goal is to clarify these ideas in an accessible way for early-stage researchers, assuming minimal prior knowledge.

## **Zero-Lag Functional Connectivity and Traveling Waves**

**Zero-lag functional connectivity** means that two brain regions show highly correlated activity fluctuations with no time difference between them. This is the basis for identifying coherent resting-state networks (e.g. the default mode network) in fMRI – regions whose signals rise and fall together are said to be functionally connected. Historically, scientists thought such long-distance synchrony had to occur with zero phase difference (perfect simultaneity) to be useful for communication . Indeed, synchronous neural oscillations can enhance effective connectivity and signal transmission between areas . However, simultaneous multichannel recordings have shown that the brain rarely operates in perfectly synced flashes. Instead, the relative timing (phase) of activity between regions is **constantly shifting**, giving rise to flexible phase differences that often manifest as traveling waves . In simple terms, a traveling wave means activity peaks move across the cortex over time, rather than all areas peaking together at once.

Crucially, these traveling waves can *produce* the appearance of zero-lag FC between specific regions. How? If two areas are hit by the same wave *phase* at roughly the same time, they will both activate together and thus show high zero-lag correlation . From a **spatiotemporal** perspective, strong zero-lag connectivity reflects that regions are in phase with a propagating wave pattern, whereas lack of correlation may mean they are reached at different phases (one area’s activity crest arrives slightly later than the other’s) . In other words, areas with synchronized timing in the wave will look correlated at zero lag, while those that a wave reaches sequentially will not. This view has been directly supported by experiments: for example, in anesthetized mice, researchers observed traveling hemodynamic waves sweeping across cortex and found that pairs of regions with *longer* relative delays (one clearly leading the other) had *lower* zero-lag correlations . Conversely, regions that the wave activated almost simultaneously had strong zero-lag FC, confirming that “zero-lag” connectivity arises from propagating activity hitting those regions together . Thus, traveling waves offer a compelling explanation for the brain’s resting connectivity structure: what looks like synchronous co-activation is often the result of waves **synchronizing phases** across distant sites.

To illustrate, imagine a stadium wave (the kind seen in sports crowds) moving through fans: two sections can stand up in unison if they are the same distance ahead of the wave’s crest. In the brain, if a slow cortical wave emanates from a source, regions at similar distances from that source will fluctuate in unison (zero lag), whereas others lag behind or ahead depending on the wave’s progression . Recent research has shown that such **global waves** propagate through the brain, spanning from sensory regions to frontal association areas, and can **parsimoniously account for large-scale functional connectivity patterns** . In fact, slowly traveling infra-slow waves (on the order of 0.01–0.1 Hz) appear to organize the cortex so that widely distributed networks activate in a coordinated sequence. Rather than thinking of resting-state networks as mysterious static correlations, we now recognize them as part of a *spatiotemporal* organization: ongoing traveling waves cause sequential activation of region groups, which in snapshot analyses looks like coherent networks blinking on and off.

## **Principal Gradients and Hierarchical Functional Organization**

The brain’s functional connectivity is not random – it has an **internal organization**. One powerful way to describe this organization is through **connectivity gradients**. Using techniques like diffusion map embedding or principal component analysis on FC data, researchers have identified a **principal gradient** that captures the largest variance in connectivity patterns . This **principal functional gradient** typically runs from primary sensory-motor areas on one end to high-level transmodal association areas on the other . In practical terms, it means each cortical region can be placed on a spectrum (or gradient) according to how “sensorimotor” versus “associative” its connectivity profile is. Primary sensory and motor cortices lie at one extreme of this gradient, highly connected with each other but not with the opposite extreme – the default mode network and other “task-negative” or introspective networks . The middle of the gradient consists of intermediate areas and multimodal integration hubs.

This principal gradient is thought to reflect a **hierarchical organization** of function in the cortex . In classic frameworks, dating back to Mesulam (1998), the cortex was proposed to be organized along a hierarchy from **unimodal** (primary sensory/motor processing) to **transmodal** (higher-order cognition) regions . The gradient findings echo this: the principal gradient essentially maps this sensory-to-association hierarchy onto a continuous axis . Early-stage researchers can imagine the gradient as the brain’s **big picture axis** – one end anchored in concrete, immediate processing (vision, hearing, movement) and the other in abstract, integrative processes (memory, self-referential thought, planning). Because it accounts for a large portion of connectivity variance, this gradient provides a unifying framework for many known networks. Rather than treating each resting-state network as an isolated module, we see them as occupying positions along a smooth gradient of functional specialization.

Notably, recent work has linked traveling waves to this principal gradient. In human fMRI, global slow waves associated with fluctuations in arousal were found to travel along the cortex in an **orderly sequence from unimodal to transmodal regions**, essentially recapitulating the principal connectivity gradient . The wave “phase map” (which regions lead or lag) showed a striking correspondence with the principal FC map: regions’ phase in the traveling wave was highly correlated with their position on the unimodal-to-transmodal gradient . This suggests that the hierarchical gradient isn’t just an abstract connectivity statistic – it has a dynamic embodiment as a traveling wave path. The brain may spontaneously propagate activity from low-level areas to high-level areas (and back), effectively **linking functional systems in a hierarchical sequence** over time . Thus, the principal gradient provides a spatial template for these waves, and the waves in turn integrate the brain along this template. We can say the cortex’s **hierarchical functional gradients** (including the principal gradient and secondary ones) are the “roads” along which activity waves travel, organizing the timing of activation across networks in a meaningful way.

## **Cortical Eigenmodes and Brain Eigenpatterns**

Another key concept for understanding functional connectivity is the idea of **cortical eigenmodes**. In physical systems, eigenmodes are the natural patterns in which a system can oscillate or resonate. For the brain, one can think of eigenmodes as the brain’s “preferred” spatial patterns of activity, determined by its anatomy and physiology. More formally, cortical eigenmodes can be defined as the eigenvectors of some operator or matrix representing the brain’s structure (for example, the Laplacian of the anatomical connectivity network) . Intuitively, these are the fundamental **standing wave patterns** the cortex can support. An analogy can be made to musical instruments: the cortex has certain “notes” it can naturally play – these are the eigenmodes – whereas typical analysis techniques (like independent component analysis or PCA of fMRI data) often identify “chords” or combinations of notes that frequently occur . As Müller and colleagues put it, the eigenmodes are analogous to the independent **notes** of a musical instrument (the brain), while common fMRI networks or components are like familiar chords composed of those notes . This analogy underscores that eigenmodes offer a **physically grounded basis** for describing brain activity, rooted in the brain’s geometry and wiring, rather than purely statistical groupings .

What do these cortical eigenmodes look like, and why do they matter for functional connectivity? Recent research on “connectome harmonics” provides some insight. **Connectome harmonics** are the eigenmodes of the brain’s structural network (often derived from diffusion MRI-based tractography). When Atasoy et al. (2016) computed these harmonic modes, they found that certain eigenmodes corresponded closely to known resting-state networks . For example, one particular eigenmode – a low-frequency harmonic of the connectome – showed a spatial pattern highly similar to the default mode network . Other low-order eigenmodes matched the visual and somatomotor networks . In general, lower-frequency (smoother) eigenmodes tended to map onto primary sensory and limbic networks, whereas higher-order cognitive networks (like executive or attention networks) were not represented by a single mode but rather by a **combination** of multiple eigenmodes . In fact, those higher cognitive networks engaged a broad range of harmonics distributed across the spatial frequency spectrum . This means complex networks are like chords comprised of several “notes” (eigenmodes), whereas simpler networks correspond to single harmonic notes.

The significance of cortical eigenmodes is that they provide a *basis set* for constructing any brain activation pattern . Just as any sound can be composed from a sum of musical notes, any spatial pattern of brain activity or connectivity can be described as a combination of these eigenmodes. This perspective helps unify structure and function: the structural eigenmodes act as **constraints or scaffolds** for functional connectivity. The brain’s spontaneous activity patterns (and hence the FC we measure over time) are not arbitrary but are biased toward these intrinsic modes. In practice, this is why we see recurring network patterns: they are built from the underlying eigenmodes of the system. Thinking in terms of eigenmodes also connects to the traveling waves discussion – a traveling wave across the cortex can be mathematically described as a mixture of eigenmodes (like how a propagating wave on a guitar string can be described by its harmonic modes). Thus, eigenmodes link the static architecture (connectome) to dynamic patterns (oscillations and waves) and to the emergent functional connectivity networks. They are a bridge between **structure and function**, giving a principled way to understand why certain FC patterns (like the principal gradient or familiar networks) are observed. As one Frontiers article argued, eigenmodes form a *physical basis* for large-scale brain activity, offering a more mechanistic framework than treating resting networks as black-box statistical results .

## **Metastability of Brain Dynamics**

The brain is often described as operating in a regime of **metastability**. What does this term mean? In dynamical systems, **metastability** refers to a state of a system that is not permanently stable, but rather **hovers among multiple possible states**, never settling into one indefinitely . It’s a bit like water that is about to boil – it has not yet transitioned fully, but it’s simmering with fluctuations between phases. In the context of brain networks, metastability means the brain continuously switches between different patterns of activity (different network configurations) over time . It is neither completely synchronized (all regions locked into one rhythm or state) nor completely independent (regions acting in isolation); instead, it shows *intermittent* synchrony and ever-changing coalitions of regions working together. One way scientists quantify metastability is by measuring the variability of the global synchronization level: for example, using the **Kuramoto order parameter** $R(t)$ to index how in-sync the phases of all brain regions are at time $t$. If $R(t)$ is consistently high (near 1), the system is in a stable synchronized state; if $R(t)$ is always low (~0), the system is in a stable asynchronous state. The brain, however, shows a $R(t)$ that **fluctuates** widely – sometimes more synchronized, sometimes less – and the standard deviation of $R(t)$ over time is taken as a measure of metastability . A high standard deviation means the level of synchrony is constantly changing, indicating the presence of fleeting coordination states rather than one persistent rhythm .

Why is metastability important? It appears to be a hallmark of healthy brain function, especially in the resting-state. In fact, computational models and analyses of empirical data suggest that the human brain at rest operates near a point of **maximal metastability** – a regime where it explores the widest repertoire of states . One influential study using whole-brain models found that the best match to real fMRI data occurred when the model was tuned to the edge of a dynamical transition, where network switching (metastability) was maximal . At this point, the model brain showed continual transitions among functional networks, much like the actual brain does. In simpler terms, the brain seems to **maintain a delicate balance** between integration and segregation: it doesn’t lock into one coherent state for long (which would be highly stable but inflexible), nor does it dissolve into noise (which would be no stability at all). Instead, it hovers in a metastable zone, enabling both the coexistence of multiple networks and the ability to reconfigure quickly . This metastability underlies the phenomenon of **dynamic functional connectivity**, where the correlation patterns between brain regions change over minutes or even seconds. Early-stage researchers can think of metastability as the brain’s way of *keeping its options open*: it has many possible patterns available and is constantly moving through them, rather than choosing one fixed ensemble of regions to synchronize permanently. Notably, metastability has been linked to cognitive flexibility – a metastable brain can more easily shift into new configurations needed for different tasks or responses.

In the context of traveling waves and gradients, metastability is related in that traveling waves can prevent the brain from getting “stuck” in one synchronized state. A traveling wave causes sequential activation (introducing phase differences), which means the whole brain is never fully synchronized at zero lag – instead, it’s partially synchronized in a *time-varying* way. This supports metastable dynamics by allowing patterns to arise and fade. Thus, metastability is essentially the dynamical backdrop that allows the brain to exploit its connectivity eigenmodes and gradients: the system continually **wanders through a landscape of states** rather than sitting in one valley forever.

## **Functional Connectivity as a Potential Landscape of Eigenmodes**

A useful metaphor for tying together these concepts is the idea of an **energy landscape** or **potential landscape** for brain activity. In physics and chemistry, one imagines a landscape with hills and valleys, where each valley (low point) represents a stable state that a system tends to settle in. This idea can be applied to brain functional connectivity: the observed patterns of co-activation can be thought of as **attractor states** – local valleys in an underlying potential landscape shaped by the brain’s anatomy and intrinsic dynamics . The bottoms of these valleys correspond to the most **probable** or energetically favorable brain states, whereas hills or higher regions of the landscape correspond to less stable, higher-energy configurations. The brain’s spontaneous activity will spend more time in or around these valleys (yielding strong functional connectivity within certain groups of regions) and will less frequently climb over the hills (which would correspond to unusual or hard-to-maintain patterns of activation).

How are these valleys determined? This is where **eigenmodes** come in. The eigenmodes of the brain (particularly those of the structural connectome) effectively sculpt the landscape. They define the natural “dimensions” along which the brain can easily deform its activity pattern. Low-order eigenmodes, especially, correspond to smooth, low-energy configurations – think of these as broad, deep valleys in the landscape. Higher-order modes add more fine-grained undulations (smaller hills and valleys). Empirical studies support this picture: when researchers applied a **maximum entropy model** to brain dynamics constrained by structural connectivity, they found that the **most likely (lowest-energy) states** involved **co-activation of spatially contiguous regions that correspond to known functional systems** . In other words, well-known resting-state networks (like visual, auditory, or default-mode networks) emerged as the local minima of the modeled energy landscape . These are combinations of regions that, due to anatomy, can jointly activate with relatively low “cost” or high probability. The model’s predictions of which networks would co-activate most matched closely with actual resting-state fMRI observations . This validates the idea that structural eigenmodes and connectivity constraints give rise to an energy landscape where certain collective states are favored.

Within this framework, functional connectivity reflects the geometry of that landscape. Strongly connected sets of regions (high FC) correspond to deep valleys (stable states where those regions are co-active frequently). Meanwhile, pairs of regions that rarely co-activate (low or negative FC) lie on opposite sides of hills or in different valleys. The brain’s **metastable** dynamics can be seen as it moving about this landscape, jumping between neighboring valleys rather than staying put. Because the brain operates near a point of high metastability, it has enough energy to explore multiple valleys (transitions between networks happen), but not so much as to flatten the landscape entirely (it doesn’t just randomize; it still prefers certain patterns) . The **eigenmodes** serve as the basis of these valleys – each eigenmode can be thought of as a direction in state-space that, if strongly expressed, lowers the energy (because it aligns with the brain’s natural resonance). A **dynamic attractor** in this view might align with one eigenmode or a combination of a few. For instance, the default mode network’s co-activation could be seen as the brain sliding into the valley corresponding to the DMN harmonic (plus some others) . To leave that valley and enter another (say, a task-positive network), the brain’s activity must change configuration (climb out and descend into a different basin), which might involve a traveling wave or other global fluctuation to redistribute phase relationships.

In summary, thinking of functional connectivity as reflecting a potential landscape emphasizes that we are looking at a **structured set of possible brain states**, not a fixed wiring diagram. The structure of that landscape is generated by the brain’s eigenmodes (its built-in “basis functions”), and the current state of the brain is like a ball rolling on this landscape – spending time in deep wells (hence showing strong within-network connectivity) but also occasionally moving to adjacent wells (hence the switching dynamics of metastability) . This perspective is supported by theoretical work linking structural connectivity to energy constraints: the brain is thought to prefer low-energy states and transitions that minimize action costs, consistent with the free-energy principle and related ideas . By studying the eigenmodes, we effectively map out the landscape’s principal features; by observing metastable switching and traveling waves, we watch the brain navigate this landscape in real time.

## **Conclusion**

Cortical traveling waves and functional connectivity are two sides of the same coin – dynamics and statistics – that together provide a richer understanding of brain organization. Traveling waves demonstrate that the brain’s synchronization is *relative and patterned in time*, not just an all-or-none simultaneous event. These waves help explain why we see zero-lag functional connectivity between distant regions: they bring those regions into the same phase, uniting them in the timing of their activity . The concept of principal gradients shows that functional connectivity is *structured*, with the principal gradient encapsulating a sensorimotor-to-association hierarchy . That gradient can be seen in how waves propagate and how networks are arranged, indicating a deep link between spatial organization and dynamic sequencing . Cortical eigenmodes, in turn, provide a foundational basis for these patterns – they are the “natural tunes” of the cortex that shape both where activity tends to concentrate and how it can flow across the network .

We introduced **metastability** to highlight that the brain is never static even at rest: it continuously transitions among configurations, echoing the idea of a repertoire of states rather than one fixed connectivity pattern . Finally, by viewing functional connectivity through the lens of a **potential landscape** shaped by eigenmodes, we gain an intuitive picture: the resting brain explores a landscape of possible states, with waves and oscillations nudging it from one basin of attraction to another, all the while constrained by the “lay of the land” (its structural connectivity and modes) .

For an early-stage researcher, the take-home message is that **functional connectivity is not magic or purely statistical** – it is grounded in concrete physical processes. Zero-lag correlations are the footprints left by traveling waves organizing brain activity in space and time. Large-scale connectivity patterns (like the principal gradient or networks) reflect the brain’s anatomical and functional hierarchy, and these patterns emerge naturally from the underlying eigenmodes of the system. The brain’s spontaneous activity is best understood as a metastable dance among these modes, rather than a stationary orchestra playing a single tune. By appreciating these concepts, one can move beyond viewing resting-state connectivity as mere correlations and start seeing it as a vibrant interaction of waves, modes, and dynamic patterns – a perspective that is both conceptually clear and increasingly supported by empirical research .
