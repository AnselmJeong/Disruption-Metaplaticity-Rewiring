The neurotrophic hypothesis gained empirical traction through a series of landmark studies that demonstrated the necessity of BDNF signaling for antidepressant action. Santarelli et al. (2003) provided causal evidence by showing that the behavioral effects of antidepressants in rodent models were abolished when hippocampal neurogenesis was blocked via X-irradiation, suggesting that the generation of new neurons was a critical mediator of mood improvement. However, this interpretation was subsequently challenged by Bessa et al. (2008), who demonstrated that antidepressants could reverse depressive-like behavior in rodents even when neurogenesis was pharmacologically suppressed. This decoupling of neurogenesis from antidepressant efficacy forced a reevaluation of the neurotrophic hypothesis, shifting the focus from cellular proliferation to synaptic plasticity as the primary substrate of therapeutic action. The resolution of this apparent paradox emerged from studies that identified BDNF-TrkB signaling as the convergent pathway through which diverse antidepressant treatments—including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), tricyclics, and even electroconvulsive therapy—exerted their effects (Castrén and Rantamäki, 2010). The discovery that antidepressants directly bind to the TrkB receptor’s transmembrane domain, stabilizing its dimer configuration and facilitating BDNF signaling, provided a mechanistic bridge between the monoamine and neurotrophic hypotheses (Casarotto et al., 2020). This finding was particularly significant because it explained how drugs targeting monoamine systems could induce structural plasticity without requiring de novo neurogenesis, thereby reconciling the acute biochemical actions of antidepressants with their delayed therapeutic effects.

The network hypothesis of antidepressant action, advanced by Castrén (2013), further refined this framework by conceptualizing depression as a disorder of maladaptive circuit wiring rather than a localized neurochemical deficit. This model posited that antidepressants do not merely restore baseline function but instead reactivate juvenile-like plasticity, enabling the brain to reorganize its connectivity in response to environmental inputs. This perspective was grounded in earlier work on critical periods in neurodevelopment, where the closure of plasticity windows was shown to limit the brain’s capacity for adaptive rewiring (Hensch, 2005). Castrén’s hypothesis suggested that antidepressants effectively "reopen" these critical periods, allowing for the correction of pathological circuit configurations when paired with behavioral interventions such as psychotherapy or cognitive training. This idea was supported by studies demonstrating that fluoxetine administration in adult rodents could reinstate ocular dominance plasticity, a hallmark of juvenile brain plasticity (Maya Vetencourt et al., 2008). The network hypothesis thus provided a theoretical foundation for the clinical observation that antidepressants are most effective when combined with psychotherapy, as the drug-induced plasticity creates a permissive state for experience-dependent rewiring.
